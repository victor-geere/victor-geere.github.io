GaussianGramPSD says that a certain matrix built from every finite family of Gaussian bell curves is positive semidefinite: its energy surface is a bowl, never a saddle. The bridge claim is that this arithmetic statement implies FiniteWeilPositivity, a statement about the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. This page lets you sweep the parameters, orbit the bowl, and read the same idea at three levels.

A family of bells, and a bowl that never dips below zero

A Gaussian is the smooth bell shape $y=e^{-x^2}$. A Gaussian family is several identical bells, all with the same width $\sigma$, sitting at different centres $a_1,\dots,a_n$ on a ruler.

To compare the bells, we fill a table $G$ called a Gram matrix. The entry in row $i$, column $j$ records a pairwise measurement of bell $i$ and bell $j$. The diagonal records a bell measured against itself. The off-diagonal entries record how strongly two different bells are linked.

The table is positive semidefinite (PSD) when every possible mix of the bells has a quadratic form — an “energy” — that is at least zero: $$Q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Gx\ge 0 \quad\text{for every real vector }x.$$ Here $x_1,\dots,x_n$ are the mixing weights. Picture $Q$ as the height of a surface over the space of mixes. PSD means the surface is a bowl whose bottom sits at zero. It is never a saddle — a surface that curves up one way and down another — letting you walk downhill.

GaussianGramPSD is the claim that this bowl property holds for every finite family, at every width and every spacing. It is not a statement about one lucky picture.

Why the bridge matters. The same table can be approached from two sides. The Gaussian side measures the bells. The zero side measures the nontrivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function. The explicit formula is the dictionary between them. The intended bridge is: if the Gaussian table is always a bowl, the zero-side table is always a bowl. That zero-side statement is FiniteWeilPositivity. In this finite Gaussian formalisation it is equivalent to the Riemann Hypothesis.

Words you can now use

  • Gaussian: a bell-curve shape.
  • Family: a finite list of bells.
  • Width $\sigma$: how fat each bell is.
  • Gram matrix: the table of pairwise links.
  • PSD: every mix has nonnegative energy.
  • Eigenvalue: a number that tells you how the bowl curves in one special direction.

Careful

PSD does not mean every entry is positive. A perfectly good bowl can have negative off-diagonal entries. The eigenvalues are what decide.

The matrix, the two kernels, and the transfer of positivity

Fix a width $\sigma>0$ and a finite family of centres $a_1,\dots,a_n$. The Gaussian test functions are $\phi_a(u)=e^{-(u-a)^2/(2\sigma^2)}$. Their reflected convolution is again a Gaussian: $$\phi_a*\check\phi_b(v)=h_{ab}(v)=\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma\exp\!\left(-\frac{(v-(a-b))^2}{4\sigma^2}\right).$$

The Weil functional $W(c,\sigma)$ assigns a real number to the reflected convolution $h_c$ of two Gaussians separated by $c$. It has three pieces: $$W(c,\sigma)=W_{\rm pole}(c,\sigma)+W_{\rm dens}(c,\sigma)+W_{\rm prime}(c,\sigma),$$ where $$W_{\rm pole}=4\pi\sigma^2e^{\sigma^2/4}\cosh\!\left(\frac c2\right),$$ $$W_{\rm dens}=\sigma^2\int_{\RR}e^{-\sigma^2t^2}\cos(ct)\,\Omega(t)\,dt,$$ $$W_{\rm prime}=-2\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\,h_{c0}(\log n),\qquad h_{c0}(v)=\sqrt{\pi}\,\sigma e^{-(v-c)^2/(4\sigma^2)}.$$ Here $\Omega(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(\tfrac14+\tfrac{it}{2})-\log\pi$, with $\psi$ the digamma function (the logarithmic derivative of the Gamma function), and $\Lambda(n)=\log p$ if $n$ is a power of a prime $p$, otherwise $\Lambda(n)=0$. The pole, density and prime pieces are the contributions of the poles at $s=0,1$, the archimedean (Gamma-factor) term, and the prime powers, respectively.

Symmetrise over the two orderings to get the Gaussian Gram matrix $$G_{ij}(\sigma)=\frac12\left(W(a_i-a_j,\sigma)+W(a_j-a_i,\sigma)\right) =\operatorname{gaussianGramEntry}(a_i-a_j,0,\sigma).$$ Then $$\boxed{\ \mathrm{GaussianGramPSD}:\quad \forall\sigma\in\RR,\ \forall n,\ \forall a_1,\dots,a_n\in\RR,\quad G(\sigma)\succeq0.\ }$$ The Lean definition quantifies over every real $\sigma$; the formulas depend only on $\sigma^2$, so the geometric width case is $\sigma>0$. Equivalently, every eigenvalue of $G$ is nonnegative, or $x^{\mathsf T}Gx\ge0$ for every real vector $x$.

On the zero side, define $$H_c(s)=2\pi\sigma^2e^{\sigma^2(s-\frac12)^2}e^{-c(s-\frac12)}.$$ For a finite set $S$ of nontrivial zeros in the critical strip, set $$B_{ij}=\sum_{\rho\in S}\operatorname{Re}H_{a_i-a_j}(\rho).$$ FiniteWeilPositivity says $B\succeq0$ for every finite $S$ and every finite Gaussian family.

The bridge is the explicit formula. In its corrected infinite form, for each $c$ the infinite sum of $\operatorname{Re}H_c(\rho)$ over all nontrivial zeros in the strip converges to $\operatorname{gaussianGramEntry}(c,0,\sigma)$. That identifies the zero-side quadratic form with the Gaussian one. So if the Gaussian matrix is PSD for every family, the zero-side matrix is PSD for every family: positivity transfers. The formal version of this transfer has a correction, given in the PhD panel.

Finite samples are not a proof

The formal statement, and which arrow is actually proved

In Lean, the arithmetic hypothesis is exactly: 

/-- Every finite Gaussian family has a PSD Gram matrix. -/
def GaussianGramPSD : Prop :=
  ∀ (σ : ℝ) (ι : Type) [Fintype ι] (a : ι → ℝ),
    (Matrix.of (fun i j ↦
      PrimeSpectrumTensor.gaussianGramEntry (a i - a j) 0 σ)).PosSemidef

Here ι is a finite index type, a assigns a real centre to each bell, σ is the common width, and PosSemidef is Lean's positive-semidefinite property. The zero-side predicate is FiniteWeilPositivity, with finiteWeilGramReal built from gaussianZeroTest. Both RH → FiniteWeilPositivity (T20) and FiniteWeilPositivity → RH (T57) are Lean-checked, so FiniteWeilPositivity ↔ RH is unconditional.

The requested bridge arrow appears as T119: finiteWeilPositivity_of_gaussianInputs has the shape GaussianExplicitFormula → GaussianGramPSD → FiniteWeilPositivity. But T121 (finiteFormula_forces_zero) shows that the finite-S version of GaussianExplicitFormula is vacuous: taking S = ∅ (the empty set) forces the arithmetic side to be zero for every c. T119 is therefore formally true but conditional on a vacuous hypothesis, so it is not a usable reduction.

The corrected input is GaussianExplicitFormulaInfinite, stated with HasSum over all nontrivial zeros in the strip. Under it, T125 proves FiniteWeilPositivity → GaussianGramPSD, via T122–T124 (PSD is a closed condition and passes to pointwise limits). T126 derives RH → GaussianGramPSD; T127 packages the three results. The converse GaussianGramPSD → FiniteWeilPositivity needs a truncation-positivity input and is not available.

So the headline arrow is the intended conceptual bridge, and T119 is its named-input shape, but the corrected formal implication runs the other way under the infinite formula. GaussianGramPSD itself remains open (RH-strength). Nothing on this page claims RH.

Headline direction

What the Gaussian bridge is asking for

Prove the arithmetic PSD statement, match it to the zero side using the explicit formula, and FiniteWeilPositivity follows. This is the conceptual shape of T119, with GaussianExplicitFormula as the matching input.

Because T121 shows the finite formula is vacuous, the corrected bridge T125 uses the infinite formula and proves FiniteWeilPositivity → GaussianGramPSD. The requested direction needs a truncation-positivity input, which is not available.

04 / definitions

Every term used above.

No external vocabulary is assumed. These are the working definitions for this page.

Gaussian
A bell-shaped function proportional to $e^{-x^2}$. In this page a Gaussian is a smooth test shape, not a probability distribution.
Gaussian family
A finite list of Gaussian bells with a common width $\sigma$ and centres $a_1,\dots,a_n$.
Width $\sigma$
The positive number controlling how quickly a bell decays away from its centre. Larger $\sigma$ means a flatter, wider bell.
Gram matrix
A square table $G$ whose entry $G_{ij}$ measures the pairing of object $i$ with object $j$. This page's objects are Gaussian test functions.
Quadratic form
The number $Q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Gx$ obtained from a matrix $G$ and a real vector $x$. It is the “energy” of the mix with weights $x$.
Positive semidefinite (PSD)
A symmetric matrix $G$ is PSD when $x^{\mathsf T}Gx\ge0$ for every real vector $x$. Equivalently, every eigenvalue is ≥ 0.
Eigenvalue and eigenvector
A number $\lambda$ and nonzero vector $v$ with $Gv=\lambda v$. Along an eigenvector, the energy is $\lambda$ times the squared length. For a symmetric matrix, the eigenvalues are real.
Riemann zeta function
The function $\zeta(s)$ initially defined by the convergent series $\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s}$ for $\operatorname{Re}s>1$ and continued analytically to the rest of the complex plane.
Completed zeta function
A symmetrised version of $\zeta$ that includes the Gamma factor and satisfies a functional equation. Its zeros in the critical strip are the nontrivial zeros.
Nontrivial zero
A complex number $\rho$ with $\zeta(\rho)=0$ that lies in the critical strip, excluding the trivial zeros at negative even integers. Equivalently, a zero of the completed zeta function in $0<\operatorname{Re}s<1$.
Critical strip / critical line
The critical strip is $0<\operatorname{Re}s<1$. The critical line is $\operatorname{Re}s=1/2$.
Riemann Hypothesis
The statement that every nontrivial zero lies on the critical line.
Explicit formula
An identity relating a sum over the nontrivial zeros to arithmetic data: here, the Gaussian Gram entry built from poles, the archimedean density and prime powers.
Weil functional
The real-valued assignment $W(c,\sigma)$ to the reflected convolution $h_c$ of two Gaussians separated by $c$. It is decomposed in the undergraduate panel into pole, density and prime terms.
Pole term
The contribution from the poles of the completed zeta function at $s=0$ and $s=1$.
Density term / archimedean term
The contribution from the Gamma factor, written with $\Omega(t)=\operatorname{Re}\psi(\tfrac14+\tfrac{it}{2})-\log\pi$, where $\psi$ is the digamma function.
Prime term
The contribution from prime powers, weighted by the von Mangoldt function $\Lambda(n)$.
Von Mangoldt function $\Lambda$
$\Lambda(n)=\log p$ when $n=p^k$ for a prime $p$ and integer $k\ge1$, and $\Lambda(n)=0$ otherwise.
FiniteWeilPositivity
The statement that the real part of the finite zero-side Gram matrix is PSD for every finite set of nontrivial zeros and every finite Gaussian family.
GaussianGramPSD
The statement that the Gaussian Gram matrix is PSD for every width and every finite Gaussian family.
Lean / formalisation
Lean is a proof assistant. A formalisation is a statement and proof written in a form a computer can check. A theorem marked “Lean-checked” has such a machine-checked proof.
HasSum
Lean's predicate saying that an infinite sum converges to a specified value in the sense of a limit of finite partial sums — finite initial pieces of the infinite sum.
Vacuous hypothesis
A condition that can never be satisfied, or whose assumed universal form forces an unwanted conclusion. A theorem conditional on such a hypothesis is formally true but carries no usable content.
Symmetric matrix
A square matrix equal to its transpose: $G_{ij}=G_{ji}$ for every $i,j$. Real symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues.
Transpose $x^{\mathsf T}$
The row obtained by turning a column vector $x$ on its side. Thus $x^{\mathsf T}Gx$ is a single number.
Reflected convolution
The pairing $\phi_a*\check\phi_b$ of a Gaussian with the reflection of another. For Gaussians it is again a Gaussian, centred at $a-b$.
Complex number / complex plane
A number of the form $a+bi$, where $a$ and $b$ are real and $i^2=-1$. The complex plane is the two-dimensional picture of all such numbers.
Gamma function
A standard complex function $\Gamma(z)$ that extends the factorial: $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ for nonnegative integers. Its logarithmic derivative is the digamma function.
Pole
A point where a complex function blows up to infinity. The completed zeta function has poles at $s=0$ and $s=1$, giving the pole term.
Digamma function $\psi$
The logarithmic derivative of the Gamma function: $\psi(z)=\Gamma'(z)/\Gamma(z)$, extended to complex arguments away from its poles.
Gamma factor
The factor built from the Gamma function in the completed zeta function. Its contribution to the Weil functional is the density term.
Analytic continuation
The unique extension of a complex function beyond its original domain in a way that remains differentiable in the complex sense. It is how $\zeta(s)$ is defined beyond the series $\sum n^{-s}$.
Functional equation
An identity relating a function at $s$ to its value at a reflected point, here the symmetry of the completed zeta function about the critical line.
Principal submatrix
The smaller matrix obtained by keeping some rows and the same-numbered columns. If a matrix is PSD, every principal submatrix is PSD.
Pointwise limit
A limit in which each matrix entry separately converges. The corrected bridge uses this to pass from finite zero-side matrices to the infinite Gram matrix.
Truncation positivity
A condition saying that PSD survives a controlled passage from finite zero sets to the full infinite set. The corrected bridge does not supply the converse arrow without such an input.
Weil criterion
The equivalence, in this finite Gaussian formalisation, between FiniteWeilPositivity and the Riemann Hypothesis. T20 gives one direction and T57 the other.
RH
Abbreviation for the Riemann Hypothesis.
Fintype
Lean's marker saying that the set labelling the bells is finite, so the family has finitely many bells.
