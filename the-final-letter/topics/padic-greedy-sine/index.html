p-adic greedy sine

A finite experiment: correcting the p-adic step sequence and testing the zeros

Status. Finite formal core and exact computation layer. The proposed step sequence p−n is not p-adically null; the corrected construction uses base-p digits (equivalently, steps pn). No relation to the Kubota–Leopoldt p-adic zeta function is asserted or found. The full dossier is in README.md.

The question

Can a p-adic analogue of the greedy harmonic sine be used in place of the Euler product over primes? The proposal was to work on Zp with steps p−n. The first finding is that this step sequence does not converge p-adically at all. The corrected object is the base-p digit expansion, and the p-adic sine is the standard power series. This experiment defines that object, computes its first zeros, and asks whether it has any relation to ζp.

The step correction

The p-adic norm is |q|p = p−vp(q). Consequently,

|p−n|p = pn → ∞,
|pn|p = p−n → 0.

So p−n are the large p-adic numbers, not small ones. An infinite greedy sum of p−n terms has no reason to converge. The p-adic analogue of “small steps” is pn, or more generally the digits an pn with an ∈ {0, …, p−1}.

Central correction. The archimedean intuition that 1/pn is small is exactly wrong in the p-adic metric. The proposed p−n sequence grows, so the original greedy algorithm cannot be transported literally.

Findings

1. The base-p greedy expansion is exact

For x ∈ Zp,

TN(x) = x mod pN,   x − TN(x) ∈ pN Zp,   |x − TN(x)|p ≤ p−N.

This is the p-adic analogue of the greedy expansion: at each step the digit is forced, and the expansion is exact in the limit. Unlike the archimedean greedy harmonic sine, there is no approximation theorem to prove; the digit expansion is exact after N digits modulo pN.

2. The p-adic sine is local, not global

The series sinp(z) = Σ (−1)k z2k+1/(2k+1)! converges for |z|p < p−1/(p−1). For odd p this disk contains p Zp; for p=2 the radius is 1/2. There is no element π ∈ Qp, so the classical Euler sine product has no direct p-adic analogue: the zeros nπ do not exist in Qp in the same way.

3. The first zeros are at the boundary, not in Qp

The Newton polygon is computed from vp(1/(2k+1)!) = −vp((2k+1)!). For the tested odd primes p = 3, 5, 7, 11, the first slope is −1/(p−1), and every nonzero slope is fractional. A nonzero root would have valuation equal to minus one of those slopes, hence fractional; no nonzero root lies in Qp. The roots are in a ramified extension on the boundary of the convergence disk. A finite search in p Zp modulo p4 finds only 0 for p = 3, 5, 7, consistent with this.

For p = 2, the first slope of the truncated polygon approaches −1 as more terms are added. A root of valuation 1 is therefore not excluded, and the p=2 case is recorded as open.

4. No relation to ζp is found

The Kubota–Leopoldt function is built by p-adic interpolation, ζp(1−k) = (1−pk−1)ζ(1−k), not by a product over primes. The zeros of sinp are boundary/ramified objects of p-adic analysis; they are not the zeros of ζp. The established local analogue of the archimedean sine factor in the functional equation is the p-adic Gamma function, not a greedy p-adic sine. No identity relating the greedy p-adic sine to ζp is proposed here; the experiment is negative on that point.

5. The “greedy” part is not the interesting content

The base-p digit expansion is exact and forced; there is no nontrivial greedy selection to study. The interesting object is the p-adic sine power series and its zeros, which is standard p-adic analysis. The experiment therefore does not supply a new route to the Euler product; it supplies a precise reason why the naive p−n proposal fails.
