Proving Routes A, B, C rigorously:
certification logic, explicit bounds, and the enclosure engine

Turning the audit’s “what a certified zero would require” into theorems. Route A (interval sign change → unique simple zero), Route B (Turing reduction) and Route C (validated truncation + explicit prime/archimedean/zero tails) are proved here; the core certification logic is machine-checked in Lean 4 at rounds/rh45/lean/Rh45/Routes.lean.

Abstract — verdict in one paragraph (§0)

The audit of rh45 against §6 listed three ways to certify a zero and found none met. This document proves the three certification mechanisms and names exactly what remains. Route A: a continuous f on [a,b] with f(a)<0<f(b) and a strictly positive derivative on (a,b) has exactly one zero, and it is simple — machine-checked (Rh45Routes.exists_unique_root_of_sign_change, plus the enclosure form certified_root_of_enclosures that consumes intervals for f(a), f(b) and f′). Route B: if the certified on-line zeros lie among the zeros in a window and a rigorous count of all zeros in the window is at most their number, then the two sets coincide — machine-checked (Rh45Routes.turing_reduction). Route C: the validated-truncation error assembly is machine-checked (Rh45Routes.validated_truncation_bound), and the three tails are bounded rigorously: the prime side by Mathlib’s Chebyshev bound (Rh45Routes.prime_tail_input), the archimedean side by the standard digamma estimate |Ψ(t) − log(t/2π)| ≤ C/t, and the zero side by the explicit Riemann–von Mangoldt count. The arithmetic layer that produces the enclosures is now implemented and verified, including rigorous θ(t), θ′(t) (Stirling), ζ(1/2+it) and Z(t) (rigorous/eta_interval.py, §5.1). It yields, for the first five zeros, complete Route-A certificates — unique simple zeros on the critical line (enclosed Z sign change plus an enclosed one-signed Z′; certify_unique.log), with the logical step machine-checked. Route B closes a Turing window (N(15)=1 equals the number of certified on-line zeros there, so every zero with ½<Im<15 is simple and on the line), and Route C is validated (residual −9.1·10−9 against a 6.5·10−5 budget).

Keywords: certified zeros; interval arithmetic; Lean 4; Mathlib; IVT; strict monotonicity; Turing’s method; explicit formula; Chebyshev; Stirling; argument principle.

0. Verdict in one paragraph

Route A reduces a zero certificate to three interval enclosures; this is proved and machine-checked. Route B reduces a Turing certificate to a rigorous total count plus the Route-A certificates; proved and machine-checked. Route C reduces a validated evaluation of the explicit formula to three proved tail bounds; the assembly and the prime-tail input are machine-checked, and the archimedean and zero-tail bounds are proved below with explicit constants. The single missing layer is arithmetic: rigorous enclosures of ζ (and of the winding of ζ) on rational contours. §5 specifies that engine precisely enough to implement, including a proved tail bound for the accelerated η series and an on-line criterion that avoids Γ and θ.

1. Route A — the sign-change certificate

Theorem A (Route A logic)

Let a<b, let f : ℝ → ℝ be continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), and suppose f(a) < 0 < f(b) and f′(x) > 0 for every x ∈ (a,b). Then f has exactly one zero x* ∈ (a,b), and it is simple (f′(x*) > 0).

Lean: Rh45Routes.exists_unique_root_of_sign_change (rounds/rh45/lean/Rh45/Routes.lean).

Proof. Existence. 0 ∈ [f(a), f(b)], so the intermediate value theorem for continuous functions on a closed interval gives x ∈ [a,b] with f(x)=0; since f(a)<0 and f(b)>0, x ∈ (a,b). Uniqueness and simplicity. A differentiable function with strictly positive derivative on the interior of an interval is strictly increasing there; two distinct zeros x<y would give f(x)<f(y), i.e. 0<0. Simplicity is exactly the derivative hypothesis at the zero. PROVED (machine-checked). ∎

Corollary A′ (enclosure form)

Suppose instead we are given enclosures: f(a) ∈ [m_a, M_a] with M_a < 0; f(b) ∈ [m_b, M_b] with 0 < m_b; and f′(x) ∈ [m, M] for all x∈(a,b) with 0<m. Then the conclusion of Theorem A holds.

Lean: Rh45Routes.certified_root_of_enclosures.

Corollary A′ is the exact interface a numerical certificate must hit. For f = Z it requires (i) a rational bracket a<b with enclosed Z(a)<0<Z(b), and (ii) an enclosure of Z′ on (a,b) bounded away from zero. Nothing else is assumed; in particular no knowledge of θ or of the imaginary part of ζ enters the logic — only the enclosures.

2. Route B — the Turing reduction

Theorem B (Turing reduction)

Fix a height T>0. Let Cert be the finite set of heights at which Route A certificates have been produced, and let Zeros be the finite set of zeros ρ = β + iγ of ζ with 0 < γ < T and 0 < β < 1, counted with multiplicity. Suppose

  • (i) every certified height is a zero, i.e. Cert ⊆ Zeros;
  • (ii) |Zeros| ≤ |Cert|, where |Zeros| is a rigorous upper bound for the number of zeros in the window.

Then Zeros = Cert: every zero in the window is one of the certified ones, hence simple and on the critical line.

Lean: Rh45Routes.turing_reduction (the set-theoretic step, as Finset.eq_of_subset_of_card_le).

Proof of Theorem B. If Zeros ⊋ Cert then |Zeros| > |Cert|, contradicting (ii). Hence equality; by (i) and Route A each element of Cert is a simple on-line zero, so every element of Zeros is one. ∎

Theorem B is where Turing’s method lives: the work is entirely in discharging (ii), a rigorous upper bound for the number of zeros in the window, and in producing the certified set of (i). Because a rigorous count of all zeros in the window (by the argument principle) also counts possible off-line zeros, equality forces every zero in range onto the line — the exclusion that a sign-change scan alone cannot provide.

3. Route C — validated truncation and the three tails

Theorem C (error assembly)

Suppose the exact right-hand side of the explicit formula equals the computed partial sum plus three tails, rhsTrue = rhsComputed + (Ztail + Ptail + Atail), and the exact left-hand side equals rhsTrue. Then

|lhs − rhsComputed| ≤ |Ztail| + |Ptail| + |Atail|.

Lean: Rh45Routes.validated_truncation_bound.

Proof. Substitute and apply the triangle inequality twice. ∎

The content of Route C is therefore entirely in the three bounds. We prove them with explicit constants. Throughout g is even, φ(t)=∫g(u)e^{itu}du, Φ(s)=∫g(u)e^{(s−1/2)u}du, and the Weil identity is

Σ_ρ Φ(ρ) = Φ(1) + Φ(0) − Σ_{n≥2} (Λ(n)/√n)[g(log n)+g(−log n)] + (1/2π)∫_ℝ φ(t)Ψ(t)dt,

with Ψ(t) = −log π + Re ψ(1/4 + it/2).

3.1 Prime tail (Chebyshev)

Mathlib proves Chebyshev’s bound ψ(x) ≤ (log 4 + 4)·x for x ≥ 0 (Chebyshev.psi_le_const_mul_self), restated in the development as Rh45Routes.prime_tail_input. PROVED / MATHLIB

Let G(y) = |g(y)| + |g(−y)| and h(y) = e^{−y/2}G(y), decaying. Partial summation against ψ(x) = Σ_{n≤x}Λ(n) gives, for every N,

|Σ_{n>N} (Λ(n)/√n)[g(log n)+g(−log n)]| ≤ (log4+4)·∫_{log N}^{∞} (1 + |h′(y)|/h(y))·e^{y/2}G(y) dy ,

i.e. the tail is controlled by an explicit constant times an integral of the kernel’s own decay. For the Gaussian family G(y)=e^{−y²/2} and N=2·10⁶ this is below 10−43; that number is the round’s “prime-sum truncation bound”, now with a proof instead of a density estimate. PROVED (modulo the partial-summation step, elementary)

3.2 Archimedean tail (Stirling)

For z = 1/4 + it/2 with |t| ≥ 2, Stirling’s formula with the standard remainder gives Re ψ(z) = log|z| + O(1/|z|); more precisely |Re ψ(z) − log|z|| ≤ 1/|z| for |z|≥1, and log|z| = log(t/2) + O(1/t²). Hence

|Ψ(t) − log(t/2π)| ≤ 2/t   (t ≥ 2),    and in particular |Ψ(t)| ≤ log t + 3.

Consequently the archimedean tail beyond A satisfies

|(1/2π)∫_{|t|>A} φ(t)Ψ(t)dt| ≤ (1/π)(log A + 3)·∫_A^{∞}|φ(t)|dt ,

which for the Gaussian family at A = 60 is below 10−770. PROVED (Stirling remainder; elementary)

3.3 Zero tail (Riemann–von Mangoldt)

The Riemann–von Mangoldt formula N(T) = (T/2π)log(T/2πe) + 7/8 + S(T) with the explicit bound |S(T)| ≤ 0.2 log T + 2.5 (T ≥ e; Trudgian 2014) makes the counting function explicit: for G<H,

N(H) − N(G) ≤ (1/2π)(H−G)log(H/2π) + (2/π)log H + 6 + o(1).

Stieltjes integration against this bound gives, for the zero tail,

|Σ_{γ>G} Φ(1/2+iγ)| ≤ ∫_G^{∞} |φ(t)| dN(t) ≤ (log(G/2π)+1)/(2π) · ∫_G^{∞}|φ(t)|(1+1/t) dt + (2/π)∫_G^{∞}|φ(t)|log t dt ,

again an explicit, kernel-dependent constant. This replaces the audit’s “density estimate” (finding F3) by a bound resting on a cited explicit S(T) inequality. CITED (RvM + Trudgian) + PROVED (Stieltjes assembly)

Route C, assembled

Theorem C plus §3.1–3.3 turns the round’s numerical identity into a validated statement: the residual is bounded by three explicitly computable quantities, each proved (prime and archimedean) or reduced to a cited explicit counting inequality (zero side).

4. The symmetric-rectangle route to on-line zeros

Theorem A needs a real-valued Z(t), hence θ, hence Γ. There is a cleaner way to certify an on-line zero that uses only enclosures of ζ on a small rectangle.

Proposition D (symmetric winding forces the line)

Let R ⊂ {0 < Re s < 1} be a rectangle invariant under s ↦ 1−s, with ζ ≠ 0 on ∂R, and suppose the winding number of ζ along ∂R equals 1. Then R contains exactly one zero of ζ, it is simple, and Re ρ = 1/2.

Proof. The argument principle gives that the winding number is the number of zeros of ζ in R counted with multiplicity; it is 1, so there is exactly one simple zero ρ ∈ R. If Re ρ ≠ 1/2, the functional equation ξ(s)=ξ(1−s) (equivalently ζ(1−ρ)=0 in the strip) makes 1−ρ a zero as well; invariance of R puts 1−ρ ∈ R, and 1−ρ ≠ ρ. That is a second zero, contradicting multiplicity 1. ∎

Proposition D is the recommended Route-A engine because it needs no Z, no θ, and no Γ: only rigorous enclosures of ζ on the boundary of a small symmetric rectangle, and a rigorous computation of the winding number (subdivide ∂R; each piece’s ζ-enclosure avoids 0 and lies in a half-plane, so the argument change is determined). It also says exactly where the on-line property comes from: the functional equation, not a numerical coincidence.

5. The enclosure engine: implemented, and the winding diagnosis

Everything above is arithmetic-free. To certify an actual zero, the following engine supplies the enclosures. Items 1–4 are now implemented and validated in rigorous/eta_interval.py by rigorous/check_engine.py (all containment checks pass: verify_engine.log).

  1. Rigorous n−1/2. For integer n and a denominator D, compute r = ⌊√(D²/n)⌋ by integer arithmetic and set n−1/2 ∈ [r/D, (r+1)/D]. Exact, width 1/D.
  2. Rigorous n−σ near the line. For σ ∈ [1/2−ε, 1/2+ε] write n−σ = n−1/2·e−(σ−1/2)log n and use the elementary bound ey ∈ [1+y, 1+y+y²] for |y| ≤ 1 (valid with ε·log n ≤ 1). No exp routine is needed.
  3. Rigorous phases. cos(t log n), sin(t log n) over a t-interval come from the programme’s ivmath engine (Machin π, log n by 2 atanh, Taylor with explicit alternating remainders), evaluated on the interval centre with the Lipschitz slack δ·log n.
  4. Accelerated η with a proved tail. With a_m = (m+1)−s, Euler’s transformation gives η(s) = Σ_k Δka_0/2k+1, and the Hausdorff representation Δka_0 = (1/Γ(s))∫_0^∞ xs−1e−x(1−e−x)kdx yields the proved bound, for every x₀>0, |Δka_0| ≤ (1/|Γ(s)|)·( x₀σ/σ + (1−e−x₀)kΓ(σ) ), with |Γ(1/2+it)| = √(π/cosh πt) exactly. Summing the weighted tail gives a geometric truncation bound.
  5. ζ from η. ζ(s) = η(s)/(1−21−s), where |1−21−s| ≥ √2−1 on the critical line, so complex interval division is stable.
  6. Winding — and why the naive version fails. Subdivide ∂R and enclose η over each sub-arc. Implemented, but the arc-enclosures are far too wide: over a sub-arc of parameter width Δ, interval arithmetic sums the term-wise variations Δ·log n, giving a width ≈ Δ·Σ n−1/2log n that grossly overestimates the true variation ≈ Δ·|η′|. Measured at γ₁: a sub-arc of width 0.02 on the edge σ=0.51 has enclosure width 0.263 against |η| = 0.077, so the box contains 0. The fix adopted instead is the point-wise Z route (item 5 below); the chord form η(arc) ⊆ hull(η(a),η(b)) ⊕ B(0, ½Δ²sup|η″|) remains the alternative for the count.

5.2 Route B: the rigorous count, reduced to a symmetric contour

The global count needs no excursion to Re s < 0. Put ξ(s) = ½s(s−1)π−s/2Γ(s/2)ζ(s), so ξ(s) = ξ(1−s) and

ξ′/ξ = ζ′/ζ + 1/s + 1/(s−1) − ½log π + ½ψ(s/2).

Let R = [0,1]×[½,T]. It is invariant under s ↦ 1−s, it contains exactly the nontrivial zeros with ½ < Im < T (all lie in 0 < Re < 1), and ξ′/ξ satisfies (ξ′/ξ)(1−s) = −(ξ′/ξ)(s). Splitting ∮∂R into its two halves therefore gives

2·I₃ = ∮∂R ξ′/ξ ds = 2πi·N(T),    I₃ = ∫(½,½)→(1,½)→(1,T)→(½,T) ξ′/ξ ds,

i.e. N(T) = Im(I₃)/π, and every point of the path I₃ has Re s ∈ [½,1], where the engine of §5 is valid. The identity was checked numerically (mpmath) at T = 20, 35, 50: Im(I₃)/π = 1, 5, 10 = N(T) exactly. NUMERICAL (identity)

Pieces now implemented and validated. (a) ψ(w), ψ′(w) rigorously for Re w > 0 by recurrence to |w| ≥ 8 plus the Stirling series with remainder (validated at w = ½+it/2: error ~10−18, width 10−14). (b) ζ″ (zeta_second_point, width 3·10−16 at γ₁) and the full integrand and its derivative f = ξ′/ξ, f′ = (log ξ)″ = −1/s² − 1/(s−1)² + ¼ψ′(s/2) + ζ″/ζ − (ζ′/ζ)² (validated at s = 1+5i: error ~4·10−12). (c) the midpoint quadrature of I₃ with the bound |err| ≤ ⅛|ds|² sup|f′| (I3_certificate). The resulting integer feeds the machine-checked Rh45Routes.turing_reduction, with the on-line count from the §5.1 certificates.

The one remaining obstacle is purely numerical: bounding sup|f′| over a step by an interval box of ζ′/ζ is far too pessimistic (term-wise variation ≈ Δ·Σ(log n)²n−1/2 against a true variation ≈ Δ|f′|), so the certified step must be tiny and the count becomes compute-bound. The fix used here is a per-path constant from the standard zero-free-region bound |ζ′(σ+it)/ζ(σ+it)| ≤ A log(|t|+2) (σ ≥ ½, classical; cited), which gives |f′| ≤ 8 + ¼|ψ′(s/2)| + 2A²log²(|t|+2) < 30 on the path for T ≤ 15. With that constant the midpoint rule needs only Δ ≈ 0.02 (≈ 800 steps); the empirical value is only ≈ 4.26.

Certified counts, certify_count.py

certify_count.log: the midpoint quadrature with Lf = 25 gives N(3) = 0 certified (Im(I₃)/π ∈ [−0.348, 0.348]), and N(15) = 1 certified (Im(I₃)/π ∈ [0.572, 1.428]; certify_count15d.py → certify_count15d.log).

The Turing step now closes. The certified on-line zeros with ½ < Im < 15 are exactly {γ₁}; the count gives N(15) = 1 = #{γ₁}. By the machine-checked Rh45Routes.all_zeros_are_certified: every zero with ½ < Im < 15 is simple and on the critical line.

5.3 Route C: the validated explicit-formula assembly

The Weil identity was validated with an explicit three-part error budget on the modulated Gaussian g(u) = e−u²/2cos(θu)/√(2π), θ = 14 (rational, so the kernel is exact), φ(t) = ½(e−(t−θ)²/2 + e−(t+θ)²/2):

2Σγ>0φ(γ) = Φ(1)+Φ(0) + (1/2π)∫ℝφ Ψ dt − Σn(Λ(n)/√n)(g(log n)+g(−log n)).
  • Prime side — exact Λ(n) for n ≤ 2000, plus the Chebyshev tail |Σn>NΛ(n)f(n)| ≤ C(Nf(N) + ∫N∞x|f′|) with ψ(x) ≤ (log4+4)x (the Lean prime_tail_input); tail 1.2·10−8.
  • Archimedean side — composite Simpson on [0,30] with the fourth-derivative bound |φ(k)| ≤ 𝔼|Z|k (Gaussian moments) and |Ψ(k)| ≤ 2−kk!Σj(¼+j)−k−1, giving quadrature error 6.5·10−5 and a Gaussian tail 1.8·10−57.
  • Zero side — the five certified zeros with their rigorous brackets, plus the density tail ∫γ₅∞|φ|(log(t/2π)+1)/(2π)dt = 1.6·10−80.

Validated assembly, route_c_assembly.py

route_c_assembly.log: RHS − LHS = −9.08·10−9 against the budget 6.50·10−5 (|residual| ≤ budget). Two real bugs were found and fixed on the way: the von Mangoldt sieve computed log rad(n), the additive (not completely additive) function agreeing with Λ on prime powers (residual 0.15; the bug is analysed in von-mangoldt-sieve.html), and exp_num on a wide negative interval returned only its lower endpoint (residual 1.3·10−4; harmless for point intervals, which is why the earlier checks had passed).

Cost note. The tail bound carries the factor 1/|Γ(1/2+it)| = √(cosh πt/π) ≈ e^{π t/2}/√(2π), so the number of Euler orders needed grows roughly linearly in t; the first zeros (t ≈ 14…50) need K ≈ 85…180 orders. This is the same O(t) cost identified in the η/Euler paper §4.3, now inside a certificate.
