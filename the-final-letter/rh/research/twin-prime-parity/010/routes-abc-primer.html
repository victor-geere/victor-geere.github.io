Routes A, B, C — a pedagogical guide to
certifying zeros of the Riemann zeta function

A self-contained explanation of the three certification strategies developed in round 010: Route A pins down one zero (existence, uniqueness, simplicity); Route B uses a rigorous count to prove that a whole window contains nothing but simple on-line zeros; Route C turns the explicit formula into a validated inequality. Everything is explained from scratch: what is being certified, why it is harder than it sounds, and how each route works.

Abstract

Suppose you want to prove — not plot, not approximate — that the first non-trivial zero of ζ lies on the critical line, that whole windows ½ < Im s < 15 and ½ < Im s < 33 contain nothing but simple on-line zeros, and that the explicit formula balances to within a stated error. Numerical evaluation alone cannot do this: a decimal expansion of ζ is not a proof, and a sign change of a floating-point partial sum can be an artefact. This paper explains, from first principles, the three strategies the round uses. Route A (existence, uniqueness, simplicity of one zero) is elementary analysis packaged for interval arithmetic: a sign change gives existence, a one-signed derivative gives uniqueness, and a non-zero derivative at the root gives simplicity. Route B (exhaustiveness in a window) is Turing’s method: a rigorous upper bound N(T) on how many zeros there can be, matched against a set of certified zeros of the same size, forces the two to coincide — a pure cardinality squeeze whose analytic input is the argument principle ∮ ξ′/ξ = 2πiN(T), reduced by the functional equation to a path in ½ ≤ Re s ≤ 1 where the interval engine is valid. Route C (a validated value) splits the explicit formula into a computed part plus three tails and bounds the residual by their sum; each tail is an inequality (Chebyshev for the primes, a Simpson-panel error for the archimedean term, the certified zero list for the zero sum). The paper states exactly which ingredient is proved (machine-checked), certified (interval arithmetic) or cited, and what none of the three routes claims: none of them proves the Riemann Hypothesis.

Keywords: Riemann zeta function; certified computation; interval arithmetic; Riemann–Siegel Z; Turing’s method; argument principle; explicit formula; Lean 4; Mathlib.

1. What does it mean to certify a zero?

1.1 The object

The Riemann zeta function is ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s, which converges for Re s > 1 and continues meromorphically to the whole plane with a single pole at s = 1. It has trivial zeros at the negative even integers and infinitely many non-trivial zeros, all in the open strip 0 < Re s < 1 (the critical strip). The critical line is Re s = ½. The Riemann Hypothesis (RH) says every non-trivial zero lies on it.

A numerical table of zeros is not a proof of anything. What we want, in this round, is a certificate: a finite body of evidence that a machine can check, from which a precise statement follows by pure logic. Three different precise statements are in play.

routestatement certifiedlogical shape
A“this bracketed interval contains exactly one zero, and it is simple” existence + uniqueness + simplicity for one zero
B“every zero with ½ < Im s < T is one of the certified simple on-line zeros” exhaustiveness: nothing is missed in the window
C“the explicit formula differs from its computed value by at most ε” a rigorous error budget for a numerical value

1.2 From complex zeros to real sign changes: the function Z

Zeros of ζ are complex, but there is a classical device that turns the search for zeros on the critical line into the search for sign changes of a real function. Put s = ½ + it and define

Z(t) = eiθ(t) ζ(½ + it),    θ(t) = arg Γ(¼ + it/2) − (t/2) log π,

with θ the Riemann–Siegel theta function chosen so that Z is real for real t. Then, for real t:

Key dictionary

ζ(½ + it) = 0  ⇔  Z(t) = 0, and the order of vanishing agrees. So zeros on the critical line are exactly the real zeros of a real smooth function Z. Since Z is real and continuous, a sign change is a zero.

1.3 Why naive computation fails

Three traps, each of which one of the routes is designed to avoid.

  • Floating point is not a proof. A computed value Z(t)≈10−9 might be 0, might be positive, might be negative — the sign is not determined by the approximation. Interval arithmetic fixes this: instead of a number you maintain an interval [lo, hi] that is guaranteed to contain the true value. If hi < 0, then Z(t) < 0 is a theorem.
  • Existence is not uniqueness. A sign change proves some zero lies between; it does not prove there is only one. Two zeros can sit in the same bracket.
  • On-line is not all-of-them. Even a perfect detection of every on-line zero up to T says nothing about zeros off the line in the same window.

Route A addresses the first two for a single zero. Route B addresses the third, and does so without ever computing a zero off the line. Route C is orthogonal: it validates a number, not a zero.

1.4 The explicit formula — what Route C actually evaluates

The Weil form of the explicit formula, in the clean audited normalisation of this round, is

∑ρ Φ(ρ) = Φ(1) + Φ(0) − ∑n≥2 (Λ(n)/√n)[g(log n) + g(−log n)] + (1/2π) ∫ℝ φ(t) Ψ(t) dt,

where Φ(s) = φ((s−½)/i), φ(t) = ∫ g(u)eitudu is the Fourier transform of an even test function g, Λ is the von Mangoldt function, and

Ψ(t) = −log π + Re ψ(¼ + it/2),   ψ = Γ′/Γ (the digamma function).

Three sums appear — over the zeros ρ, over the prime powers n, and over the real line (the archimedean term). Route C bounds each one. That is why the rounds care about ψ and its derivatives at all: they are the archimedean term.

2. Route A — pinning down one zero

2.1 The logic, in words

One zero is easy to certify, if you can certify three inequalities on the bracket [a,b]:

  1. Existence. Z is continuous on [a,b] and Z(a) and Z(b) have opposite signs. The intermediate value theorem gives a zero in (a,b).
  2. Uniqueness. Z′ is one-signed on (a,b) (say Z′ > 0). Then Z is strictly increasing, so it can cross zero at most once: the zero is unique.
  3. Simplicity. At the zero, Z′ ≠ 0 (it is already known to be > 0, or < 0). A zero with non-zero derivative has multiplicity one, and crossing the critical line transversally means the zero is simple.

Notice what is not required: no value of Z is ever needed exactly, only the signs and the sign of Z′. That is precisely what interval arithmetic can deliver.

Theorem A (Route A; Lean exists_unique_root_of_sign_change)

theorem exists_unique_root_of_sign_change
    {f : ℝ → ℝ} {a b : ℝ} (hab : a < b)
    (hf  : ContinuousOn f (Icc a b))
    (hf' : ∀ x ∈ Ioo a b, 0 < deriv f x)
    (ha  : f a < 0) (hb : 0 < f b) :
    ∃ x, x ∈ Ioo a b ∧ f x = 0 ∧ 0 < deriv f x ∧ ∀ y, y ∈ Ioo a b → f y = 0 → y = x

Read the conclusion aloud: there is a point x in the open interval with f x = 0, with f′(x) > 0 (simplicity), and any other zero in the interval equals x (uniqueness).

The proof is three classical facts glued together, and it is worth seeing the glue because that is what the formalisation contributes: 

-- existence: IVT on the sign change
obtain ⟨x, hx, hfx⟩ := intermediate_value_Icc hab.le hf ⟨ha.le, hb.le⟩
-- uniqueness: strict monotonicity from a positive derivative
have hmono : StrictMonoOn f (Icc a b) := strictMonoOn_of_deriv_pos (convex_Icc a b) hf …
-- then any second zero violates strict monotonicity (a Rolle-type contradiction)

2.2 The reality of the hypotheses: enclosures, not values

A numerical certificate never produces f a < 0 directly; it produces an interval enclosure f a ∈ [la, ha] with ha < 0. Route A is therefore also stated in the form the certificate actually supplies:

Theorem A′ (enclosure form; Lean certified_root_of_enclosures)

Given enclosures f a ∈ [la,ha] with ha < 0, f b ∈ [lb,hb] with 0 < lb, and deriv f x ≥ m on (a,b) with m > 0, there is a unique simple zero in (a,b).

The bridge from Theorem A′ to Theorem A is two linarith steps: an upper bound ha < 0 makes f a < 0, and a uniform lower bound deriv f ≥ m > 0 makes the derivative positive pointwise. This is the whole formal content of “an interval certificate is a proof”.

Pitfall: even-order zeros and the role of Z′

If Z had a double zero on the line, it would touch zero without crossing and no sign change would detect it; and at a double zero Z′ = 0, so the derivative hypothesis is exactly the instrument that rules this out. Route A therefore certifies transversal (hence simple) on-line zeros, and only those.

3. Route B — ruling out the zeros you have not seen

3.1 Turing’s idea, in words

Route A finds zeros. Route B answers the harder question: have we found all of them in a window? The idea, due to Turing (1953), is a cardinality squeeze, and it needs no new zero computed off the line:

  1. Get a rigorous upper bound N(T) on the number of zeros with 0 < Im ρ ≤ T — a bound that counts all zeros, wherever they are in the strip.
  2. Exhibit N(T) certified on-line zeros in the window, each by Route A.
  3. Then the certified set has exactly as many elements as there can possibly be zeros. Hence the two sets are equal — and every zero in the window is a certified one, i.e. simple and on the line.

The logical core is so short that it is a one-liner about finite sets:

Theorem B (Turing reduction; Lean turing_reduction, all_zeros_are_certified)

theorem turing_reduction {α} [DecidableEq α] {zeros certified : Finset α}
    (hsub  : certified ⊆ zeros)          -- every certified zero is a zero
    (hcard : zeros.card ≤ certified.card)  -- the rigorous count is at most the certified count
    : zeros = certified :=
  (Finset.eq_of_subset_of_card_le hsub hcard).symm

There is no analysis here at all. The whole work of Route B is to earn the two hypotheses: the subset relation is Route A, and the cardinality bound is the rigorous count.

3.2 Where the count comes from: the argument principle

The count N(T) is produced not by finding zeros but by integrating the logarithmic derivative of a completed ζ. Let

ξ(s) = ½ s(s−1) π−s/2 Γ(s/2) ζ(s).

The factor ½s(s−1) removes the pole, π−s/2Γ(s/2) is the archimedean factor, and the result is entire and satisfies the clean functional equation ξ(1−s) = ξ(s). Its zeros are exactly the non-trivial zeros of ζ. The argument principle for a rectangle R says

∮∂R ξ′/ξ ds = 2πi · (#zeros of ξ inside R, with multiplicity).

This is the identity that Route B turns into a count. Two simplifications make it usable:

  • Symmetry halves the contour. With ξ(1−s) = ξ(s) the integrand satisfies logDeriv ξ(1−conj s) = −conj(logDeriv ξ(s)), so the left half of the contour is the conjugate of the right half. Writing I₃ for the right-half integral, 2I₃ = ∮∂R = 2πi N(T), hence N(T) = Im(I₃)/π — only the imaginary part is needed, and the path stays in ½ ≤ Re s ≤ 1.
  • Quadrature with a budget. I₃ is evaluated by the midpoint rule with the curvature bound |err| ≤ ⅛|ds|2 sup|f′|, the step being chosen so that the total quadrature budget is below π/2 — because N(T) is an integer, an enclosure of width < 1 around Im(I₃)/π pins it down exactly.

3.3 The three hypotheses that used to be gaps — now theorems

The symmetry reduction as stated above assumes the argument principle. In earlier rounds that assumption was explicit (hAP), together with two concrete facts about ξ. All three are now proved in the same development, and this is worth stressing pedagogically, because it shows how the route became unconditional:

What Route B does not say

It says nothing about Im s ≥ T, nothing about Re s ≠ ½ outside the window, and it is not a step towards RH in the sense of a general argument — it is a finite verification, and it remains finite however far the engine is pushed. It is exactly the kind of statement (a Turing window) that makes a numerical table into a theorem for the range covered.

4. Route C — a validated value for the explicit formula

4.1 The shape of the argument

Route C is not about zeros as such; it is about turning a numerical evaluation of the explicit formula into an inequality. The pattern is always the same. Write the exact quantity as a computed partial sum plus a remainder, bound the remainder, and add:

Theorem C (validated truncation; Lean validated_truncation_bound)

theorem validated_truncation_bound
    {lhs rhsTrue rhsComputed zeroTail primeTail archTail : ℝ}
    (hlhs   : lhs = rhsTrue)
    (hsplit : rhsTrue = rhsComputed + (zeroTail + primeTail + archTail)) :
    |lhs - rhsComputed| ≤ |zeroTail| + |primeTail| + |archTail|

The proof is one line of algebra (the residual is the sum of the tails) followed by the triangle inequality twice. It is deliberately trivial: the substance is entirely in the three tail bounds, which are supplied by other lemmas.

4.2 The three tails, one at a time

tailhow it is boundedbound used

archimedeancomposite Simpson on [0,30] with the sharp panel error (2H)5/2880·sup|G(4)|, the derivative budgets coming from the polygamma series1.6·10−8

The prime tail is where Mathlib contributes a ready-made inequality: 

theorem prime_tail_input (x : ℝ) (hx : 0 ≤ x) :
    Chebyshev.psi x ≤ (Real.log 4 + 4) * x :=
  Chebyshev.psi_le_const_mul_self hx

i.e. the elementary estimate ψ(x) = ∑n≤xΛ(n) ≤ (log 4 + 4)x, which substitutes the density guess ψ(x) ≈ x of a numerical script by a proved one.

4.3 The result

Route C verdict

RHS − LHS = −9.07873·10−9 against a budget of 2.794·10−8, so |residual| ≤ budget. The test function is g(u) = e−u²/2cos(14u)/√(2π), chosen with a rational centre so that the kernel is exact. Log: rigorous/route_c_assembly.log.

Why an interval inclusion is stronger than a small number

A floating-point residual of 10−9 might be 10−9 of genuine disagreement, or an artefact of rounding. An interval enclosing each tail, summed by the triangle inequality, produces a bound that no rounding error can violate: the statement “|residual| ≤ 2.794·10−8” is true of the exact mathematical objects, whatever the machine did. This is the difference between checking a computation and proving one.

4.4 Route C’s inputs are all proved or certified

Route C itself has no cited ingredient: the error assembly is a Lean theorem (validated_truncation_bound), the prime tail is Mathlib’s Chebyshev bound (prime_tail_input), the archimedean tail rests on the machine-checked Simpson panel error and the polygamma budget, and the zero tail is the certified list. One ingredient used to be cited and no longer is, and it is worth recording where it lived, because the primer initially attributed it here: the Route B quadrature needed a curvature bound sup|f′| < 30 on the I₃ path, cited as |ζ′/ζ| ≤ A log t with A = 1. That citation has been removed — the curvature is now enclosed per step by the engine’s rigorous local Taylor enclosure zeta_local_box — so the T = 15 count is unconditional. See §3.4.

5. How the three routes interlock

Read the figure as a dependency graph:

  • A → B. Route B is useless without certificates to feed the subset hypothesis certified ⊆ zeros. Conversely a certificate alone is useless for exhaustiveness.
  • B → C. The zero tail of the explicit formula is evaluated from the certified zero list; without Route B’s window one could not claim the list is complete over the range used.
  • C is independent in method. Route C shares only the interval engine (rigorous/ivmath.py, eta_interval.py) and the archimedean budget. Its logic (triangle inequality + tail bounds) has nothing to do with zeros.
  • The shared substrate is the interval engine: every “enclosed” claim in the table above is a call into rigorous/, validated against mpmath by containment (check_engine.py, 0 fail(s)).

There is also a shared analytic substrate, and it grew during this round. Route B needed the argument principle; Route C needed the archimedean derivative budget. Both are now theorems of the Lean development rather than citations:

Follow-up: do the routes generalise beyond ζ?

Answered in round 011. Short version: the theorem cores are already general in the Lean (∀ f, ∀ Finset α), so the work is instantiation — a functional equation, conjugation reality, an interval evaluator, and an explicit formula per object. The closest second object is the Dirichlet L-function: Mathlib has its functional equation, round 011 proves the normalisation that puts it in Route B's shape, and one substantive Mathlib gap remains (conjugation reality, for which this round's completedRiemannZeta₀_conj is a template). The all-zeros generalisation is impossible by construction — that is RH.
