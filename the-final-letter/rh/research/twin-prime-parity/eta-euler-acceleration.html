The η/Euler Route to ζ on the Critical Line:
Alternation, the Odd/Even Split, and Convergence Acceleration

Everything behind the 128-term 10−15 reading in the 005/eta_digitmap_lab.html panel A: what the alternating Dirichlet η series is, how its odd and even parts evolve and cancel, what Euler's transformation does and why it is geometric, and where the method sits among Aitken Δ², Shanks, Wynn, Richardson and the Borwein/Cohen–Rodriguez Villegas–Zagier accelerators.

Subject: numerical evaluation of ζ(1/2+it) by the alternating series  ·  Status: classical algorithm, re-derived and re-measured here  ·  Evidence: exact identities (theorems) plus reproducible double- and arbitrary-precision numbers  ·  Not claimed: this document proves no zero of ζ

Abstract — verdict in one paragraph (§0)

The zero you see in the digit-map lab is not produced by the partial sum; it is produced by acceleration of a convergent alternating series. The plain Dirichlet series for ζ diverges at σ = 1/2, but the alternating series η(s) = Σ(−1)n−1n−s = (1−21−s)ζ(s) converges there, because odd and even terms cancel in adjacent pairs. That convergence is slow — error ≈ N−1/2 — so one replaces the partial sums by Euler's transformation Σ(−1)nan = Σ Δka1/2k+1, whose terms are weighted finite differences. For an = n−s the transformed terms peak during a transient of length ≈ |t| and then decay geometrically, so O(t) terms give 10−n accuracy for n digits (at t = γ1: error 5.2·10−6 at 32 terms, 3.5·10−34 at 128 terms in exact arithmetic). This places it in the family of sequence transformations (Aitken Δ², Shanks, Wynn ε, Richardson, Levin, Borwein/CRZ), all of which trade a slow tail for a fast one by modelling the error. The practical limit is not truncation but round-off in the iterated differences: in IEEE double the error bottoms out at ≈ 2·10−15, which is exactly why the lab's green reference line reads 10−15 and not zero.

Keywords: Dirichlet eta function; alternating series; Euler transformation; convergence acceleration; Aitken Δ²; Shanks transformation; Wynn epsilon algorithm; Padé approximants; Borwein algorithm; Riemann zeta function; critical line; zeros; numerical analysis.

Honest headline

Everything here is classical. The identity η = (1−21−s)ζ, the convergence of η for Re s > 0, Euler's transformation and its geometric error, and the accelerators of Aitken, Shanks and Wynn are all textbook. What this document adds is a single self-contained, re-measured account: the odd/even evolution of the partial sums, the transient then geometric behaviour of the Euler terms, the optimal-truncation / round-off floor, and the cost scaling in t. Nothing here proves that any ζ(1/2+iγ) = 0; the document says explicitly (§6) what a proof would require.

0. Verdict in one paragraph

ζ(s) has no usable Dirichlet series at σ = 1/2: its partial sums grow like N1/2/|1−s| and never settle. The alternating series η(s) = Σ(−1)n−1n−s does converge there, because adjacent odd/even pairs nearly cancel; but it converges only like N−σ. Applying Euler's transformation to that convergent alternating series turns the slow N−σ tail into a geometric one, so a few dozen to a few hundred terms yield 10−15 or better. The zeros of interest are the zeros of η (for 0 < σ < 1, exactly the non-trivial zeros of ζ), which is why the accelerated value reads zero at t = γ and O(1) off it. The same idea appears under several names — Aitken Δ², Shanks, Wynn ε, Richardson, Levin, Borwein — and the practical ceiling in double precision is round-off, not mathematics.

1. The object: ζ, the alternating series η, and why alternation helps

Definition 1.1 (the two series)

ζ(s) = Σ_{n≥1} n^{−s}                    (converges for Re s > 1)
η(s) = Σ_{n≥1} (−1)^{n−1} n^{−s}          (converges for Re s > 0)
     = 1 − 2^{−s} + 3^{−s} − 4^{−s} + …

The two are related by the classical identity η(s) = (1 − 21−s)ζ(s): for Re s > 1,

Σ (−1)n−1n−s = Σ n−s − 2·Σ (2k)−s = ζ(s) − 21−sζ(s) = (1 − 21−s)ζ(s),

and the identity extends to Re s > 0 because both sides are analytic there (the alternating series converges there, and (1−21−s)ζ(s) extends meromorphically, the pole of ζ at s = 1 being cancelled by the zero of the prefactor). At s = 1 this reads η(1) = ln 2.

1.1 Why alternation extends convergence to Re s > 0

Group the series in odd/even pairs:

η(s) = Σ_{k≥0} [ (2k+1)−s − (2k+2)−s ].

Each bracket is a difference of adjacent values of x−s, so

(2k+1)−s − (2k+2)−s = −∫2k+12k+2 s x−s−1dx,   |bracket| ≤ |s| (2k+1)−σ−1.

For σ > 0 the right side is summable, so the paired series converges absolutely; and the odd partial sums differ from the even ones by the single leftover term (2K+1)−s → 0. Hence the full alternating series converges for Re s > 0. This is the precise sense in which the convergence of η is the cancellation of odd against even terms — the theme of §2.

1.2 The zeros

For 0 < σ < 1 the prefactor 1−21−s never vanishes, so on the critical strip

η(σ+it) = 0  ⇔  ζ(σ+it) = 0.

On the critical line σ = 1/2 these are exactly the non-trivial zeros. There is one trap: 1−21−s = 0 on the whole line Re s = 1, at t = 2πk/log 2 ≈ 9.0647k. There η has zeros that are not zeros of ζ. So "the accelerated value is zero" means "a zero of η", and it means "a zero of ζ" only for σ ≠ 1.

2. The odd/even split and how the two sums evolve

This is the centre of the document. Write the odd and even partial sums separately:

Definition 2.1 (odd/even partial sums)

S_N(s) = Σ_{n≤N} n^{−s}                       (the plain ζ partial sum)
O_N(s) = Σ_{n≤N, n odd}  n^{−s}               (odd part)
E_N(s) = Σ_{n≤N, n even} n^{−s}               (even part)

S_N = O_N + E_N ,      η_N := O_N − E_N = Σ_{n≤N} (−1)^{n−1} n^{−s} .

2.1 The exact algebraic relation between the three sums

The even terms are a rescaled copy of a shorter ζ partial sum:

E_N(s) = Σ_{k≤N/2} (2k)−s = 2−s S⌊N/2⌋(s).

Therefore

O_N = S_N − 2−sS⌊N/2⌋,    η_N = S_N − 21−sS⌊N/2⌋.

The second identity is the whole mechanism. Both S_N and 21−sS⌊N/2⌋ contain the same divergent leading term, which cancels before the limit is taken.

2.2 Why the growing parts cancel (Euler–Maclaurin)

By Euler–Maclaurin, for s ≠ 1,

S_N(s) = ζ(s) + N1−s/(1−s) + ½N−s + O(N−s−1).

Substitute N → ⌊N/2⌋ and multiply by 21−s: 21−s(N/2)1−s/(1−s) = N1−s/(1−s). The two N1−s terms are identical and cancel, leaving

η_N(s) = (1−21−s)ζ(s) + O(N−σ) = η(s) + O(N−σ).

On σ = 1/2 that is an error O(N−1/2). Measured at t = γ1 the constant is 0.5: N1/2|ηN| → 0.5000 (Table 5.1).

2.3 How the two sums actually evolve

Using the same expansion in O_N = S_N − E_N,

E_N(s) ≈ N1−s/(2(1−s)),    O_N(s) ≈ N1−s/(2(1−s)),

so on the critical line

|O_N| ≈ |E_N| ≈ √N / (2|1−s|),    |S_N| ≈ √N / |1−s|,    |η_N| ≈ 0.5 / √N.

The odd and even sums grow without bound, at the same rate and with the same phase; their difference is what converges. At N = 400, t = γ1: |O_N| = 0.7072, |E_N| = 0.7081, while |η_N| = 2.499·10−2 and |S_N| = 1.4150; the guides are √N/(2|1−s|)=0.7070 and 0.5/√N = 2.50·10−2. Figure 1 shows the four trajectories on a log–log axis: two parallel ascending lines (O_N, E_N), their sum ascending twice as high (S_N), and the green difference descending with slope −1/2.

Relation to the programme's P(p)/N(p) budgets (rh42)

At σ > 1, where the plain Dirichlet series converges, the same odd/even split gives the convergent budgets P(p) = Σ(2k)−p = 2−pζ(p) (even) and N(p) = Σ(2k+1)−p = (1−2−p)ζ(p) − 1 (odd), with ζ = 1 + N + P and η = 1 + N − P. Those are index-parity budgets and, as the programme proves elsewhere, carry no Ω-parity information. Here the very same split is being used for the opposite purpose: not as a sieve, but as the cancellation that makes η computable at σ = 1/2. Do not confuse this index-parity split with rh43's sign split P_N = |M_N| (positive/negative real parts), which is a different partition of the same terms.

3. Convergence acceleration: Euler, Aitken, Shanks, Wynn

All accelerators share one idea. Given partial sums sn → s, suppose the error has a model

sn = s + c1λ1n + c2λ2n + … ,    1 > |λ1| ≥ |λ2| ≥ …

A transformation that cancels the first k error modes leaves an error of order λk+1n: "model the error, subtract it, repeat". The methods differ in the model and in the algebra used to cancel it.

3.1 Euler's transformation

Definition 3.1 (forward differences and Euler's transformation)

Δa_n = a_n − a_{n+1}                     (forward difference)
Δ^k a_n = Δ(Δ^{k−1} a_n)

Σ_{n≥0} (−1)^n a_n  =  Σ_{k≥0}  Δ^k a_0 / 2^{k+1}          (Euler, 1755)

Derivation. Let E an = an+1 be the shift. Formally Σ(−1)nan = (1+E)−1a0, and since Δ = 1 − E,

(1+E)−1 = (2 − (1−E))−1 = (2 − Δ)−1 = (1/2)(1 − Δ/2)−1 = Σ_{k≥0} Δ^k / 2^{k+1}.

Exact check on an = 1/(n+1) (so the series is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − … = ln 2): Δka0 = 1/(k+1), so Σk (1/(k+1))/2k+1 = Σm≥1 1/(m 2m) = ln 2. ∎

Why it beats plain truncation. The 2−(k+1) factor is fixed and geometric. The differences Δka0 are not small for the sequences used here — Table 5.3 shows them growing by four orders of magnitude — so it is the fixed weight, once the transient is past, that forces the decay. For a sequence with the Hausdorff representation an = ∫01 tndμ(t) (which includes an = n−s = (1/Γ(s))∫0∞xs−1e−nxdx), the transformation is just the Abel/Hausdorff identity

Σ_{n≥0} (−1)^n a_n = ∫_0^1 dμ(t)/(1+t),    Δ^k a_0 = ∫_0^1 (1−t)^k dμ(t),

and the tail after K orders is ∫01(1−t)K+1/(2K+1(1+t))dμ(t). The (1−t)K+1 concentrates the integral ever closer to t = 0, which is why convergence is geometric once the mass has localized — the transient identified in §4.3.

The catch, visible immediately. On an = n−s the raw differences do not decay. At s = 1/2 + iγ1, Table 5.3 shows |Δka1| rising from 1 to 104 over k ≤ 34; it is only the 2−(k+1) weight that turns the products Tk = Δka1/2k+1 downward. This is also why round-off matters (§4.4).

3.2 Aitken's Δ² (the simplest nonlinear accelerator)

If sn = s + cλn exactly, then eliminating s, c, λ from three consecutive partial sums gives

ŝ_n = s_n − (Δs_n)² / Δ²s_n ,    Δs_n = s_{n+1}−s_n,   Δ²s_n = s_{n+2}−2s_{n+1}+s_n.

Aitken Δ² is exact for a single geometric error mode and removes it otherwise; the residual is O(λ2n). It is the k = 1 case of the next two.

3.3 Shanks' transformation and Padé approximants

Shanks' ek(sn) is the result of assuming sn = s + Σj≤kcjλjn and solving for s; it is a ratio of (k+1)×(k+1) Hankel determinants and equals the [n+k/k] Padé approximant of the generating function of the terms. It is exact when the error is a sum of k geometrics.

3.4 Wynn's ε-algorithm

Definition 3.2 (Wynn, 1956)

ε_{−1}^{(n)} = 0 ,   ε_0^{(n)} = s_n ,
ε_{k+1}^{(n)} = ε_{k−1}^{(n+1)} + 1 / ( ε_k^{(n+1)} − ε_k^{(n)} ) .

The even columns ε2k(n) are Shanks' transforms ek(sn); the odd columns are auxiliary. The algorithm computes the whole Shanks table in O(N2) operations and is numerically more robust than solving the Hankel systems directly. Aitken Δ² is ε2.

3.5 The rest of the family

  • Richardson extrapolation models the error as a series in a known small parameter hp (e.g. step size); it is the linear-transform cousin of the above.
  • Levin's transformations and Weniger's variant use a remainder estimate ωn (e.g. ωn = an+1) and are the standard general-purpose accelerators for non-alternating, logarithmically convergent sequences.
  • Borwein's algorithm (2000) and the Cohen–Rodriguez Villegas–Zagier acceleration are purpose-built for alternating series of the type Σ(−1)nf(n): using a Chebyshev-like expansion of the terms they achieve 10−n accuracy with O(n) terms (and are widely used for ζ and Dirichlet L-functions). Euler's transformation is the oldest and simplest member of this alternating-series subfamily; the Borwein/CRZ methods are faster and more robust but structurally the same bargain.

4. The η/Euler route to ζ on the critical line

4.1 The evaluation formula

Algorithm 4.1 (η → ζ)

given s = σ + it ,  σ > 0 ,  σ ≠ 1 :

  η(s)  =  Σ_{k≥0} Δ^k a_1 / 2^{k+1} ,      a_n = n^{−s}          (Euler transform)
  ζ(s)  =  η(s) / (1 − 2^{1−s})                                   (regularisation)

For σ = 1/2 the division is harmless (|1−21−s| > 0.58), and the argument of ζ is obtained from the real and imaginary parts of the accelerated sum. This is the "η/Euler route": alternate to get convergence at σ = 1/2, accelerate to make the convergence usable, divide to recover ζ.

4.2 Accuracy, measured

At t = γ1, using exact rational/high-precision arithmetic so that only truncation survives (Table 5.2): 5.2·10−6 at K = 32, 3.96·10−15 at K = 64, 7.5·10−20 at K = 80, 3.5·10−34 at K = 128. The decay is geometric with an effective ratio ≈ 1/2 per added difference order over this range (the errors track 2−(K−17)), i.e. about one decimal digit per 3.3 added orders, far better than the raw N−1/2. Figure 2 contrasts the two.

4.3 The transient, and the cost in t

The transformed terms Tk = Δka1/2k+1 are not monotone. At γ1 they dip to about 4.9·10−2 around k = 4, rise to about 1.74·10−1 around k = 7, and only then turn down; the running sum does not settle until the transient is past. The transient's length grows with |t|, because the differences must "differentiate away" a phase that oscillates like t log n. Measured, the first difference order at which |Tk| < 10−13 is

t14.133050 100200500
first k with |Tk| < 10−13 5979101152 243488

So the term count grows roughly linearly in t (about 0.9t + 45 over this range), i.e. O(t) terms and O(t2) work for the difference table. This is the classical trade-off against the Riemann–Siegel formula, which needs only O(√t) terms (but a more delicate implementation). For |t| up to a few hundred the η/Euler route is simple and accurate; for large heights, Riemann–Siegel or an Euler–Maclaurin evaluation is preferable. At t = 800 the raw differences overflow IEEE double (circa k ≈ 1000) before the transient ends, so the method is also precision-limited at large heights.

4.4 Stability and the optimal truncation

Two error sources compete:

  • Truncation falls geometrically once past the transient, as measured in §4.2.
  • Round-off arises because Δka1 is a difference of nearly equal numbers; a perturbation ε in the terms can grow like 2kε through k levels of subtraction. The weight 2−(k+1) compensates exactly, so the propagated error is O(ε) and does not explode — but it does set a floor.

Measured in IEEE double at γ1 (Table 5.4): 3.95·10−15 at K = 64, a best of 1.75·10−15 near K ≈ 72, then a slow rise to 5.8·10−15 at K = 256. The sweet spot is K ≈ 2–3×|t| but never much past the point where truncation meets ε ≈ 10−16. Reaching 10−30 or beyond requires higher working precision (or interval arithmetic), which is exactly what one does when certifying rather than merely evaluating (§6).

6. What is proved, what is evaluated, what a zero would require

Theorems used (classical; not re-proved here)

  • T1. η(s) = (1−21−s)ζ(s), and η converges for Re s > 0 (pairing proof, §1.1). THEOREM
  • T2. Euler's transformation Σ(−1)nan = ΣΔka0/2k+1 for convergent alternating series (§3.1). THEOREM
  • T3. Shanks' transform is the ratio of Hankel determinants and the [n+k/k] Padé approximant of the term-generating function; Wynn's recursion computes it; Aitken Δ² is ε2. THEOREM
  • T4. For 0 < σ < 1, η(σ+it) = 0 ⇔ ζ(σ+it) = 0.

What the numbers are, and are not

Every table and figure here is a numerical evaluation. A small value of |η| at some t is evidence, not proof, that ζ(1/2+it) = 0: the function could be small without vanishing, and floating-point error can mask a small non-zero value. NUMERICAL

To certify a zero one needs a rigorous argument, typically one of:

  • Route A — interval arithmetic. An interval-/ball-arithmetic evaluation of ζ showing a sign change of the real function Z(t) = eiθ(t)ζ(1/2+it) on a rational bracket, followed by a root-isolation argument (a bound |Z′| > 0 on the bracket);
  • Route B — Turing's method. A rigorous count N(T) of zeros up to height T (argument principle with explicit ζ'/ζ or S(T) bounds) plus a matching number of sign changes — this also excludes missed or off-line zeros;
  • Route C — a validated explicit formula. Rigorous truncation bounds for the zero, prime and archimedean terms, so the identity is a theorem on the evaluated input.

The programme's rh45 builds all three instruments and demonstrates them numerically (zeros from Z(t) to 32–33 digits; a ζ'/ζ contour count; the corrected Weil formula to ~45 digits), but it does not yet meet the rigor of A/B/C: it evaluates with 60-digit mpmath floats rather than enclosures, its count is checked against mp.zetazero rather than used as a Turing exclusion, and its tail controls are density-style estimates on an mp.zetazero zero side. The point-by-point audit is rounds/rh45/audit.html; the three certification routes themselves are now proved rigorously in rounds/rh45/routes-abc.html.

None of those steps is the η/Euler evaluation. The evaluation is the fast, simple way to find candidate zeros; certification is a separate, costlier layer. The programme's own ledger is explicit that "locating zeros is not a statement about where they all are".

7. Failure modes and caveats

  1. The Re s = 1 factor zeros. η vanishes at s = 1 − 2πik/log 2, which are not zeros of ζ. At σ = 1.000 the reference line will read zero at t ≈ 9.0647k for the wrong reason.
  2. Division near σ = 1. ζ = η/(1−21−s) loses precision as 1−21−s → 0; use the functional equation or a direct method near the pole.
  3. Transient and term count. The number of difference orders needed grows with |t| (§4.3); a fixed K = 128 is ample at γ1 but marginal at γ3 and useless at t = 200.
  4. Round-off floor and overflow. Double precision limits the error to ≈ 2·10−15; at very large t the raw differences Δka1 overflow the double range before the transient ends.
  5. Euler's transformation accelerates a convergent series; it does not create convergence. It is valid for Re s > 0 for η, and cannot be applied to ζ's own Dirichlet series (which diverges for σ ≤ 1).
  6. Non-oscillatory, slowly convergent sequences (e.g. Σ n−s for σ near 1) are not helped by the alternating Euler transform; there one uses Levin/Weniger or Euler–Maclaurin instead.

8. The 005 digit-map lab, precisely

In 005/eta_digitmap_lab.html:

  • Panel A plots |Re η_N| and |Im η_N| — the raw partial sums — on a log axis floored at 10−9. Their vertical excursions come from the absolute value of a sign-changing quantity (and from Im η1 = 0 exactly), not from acceleration.
  • The green reference line is |η(σ+it)| from etaEuler(σ,t,128) — Euler's transformation with 128 terms, as in §3.1. It is a level marker for the current t, which is why it spans the plot at a constant height and drops to the floor exactly when the current t is a zero.
  • The digit map is orthogonal: it rewrites each term's phase e−it log n as a product over the greedy support An, leaving the radius n−σ untouched. The accelerator sees an array of (complex) terms; it does not care how their phases were produced. The measured gap between the direct and digit-map curves is the greedy truncation, ≈ 3·10−6.
  • Consequently: the lab's 5.23·10−15 is Euler acceleration at σ = 1/2, and the disappeared value is the O(N−1/2) tail of an ordinary partial sum.
