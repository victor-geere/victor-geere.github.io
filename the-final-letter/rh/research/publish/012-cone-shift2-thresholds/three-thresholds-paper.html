Abstract

Row T14 of the rh42 ledger asserts that the three thresholds attached to the “budgets” of a signed greedy expansion satisfy pN < pP < p*. The claim was previously verified only numerically, by 80-digit root-finding with residuals below 10−30. We give a complete proof, formalised in Lean 4 against Mathlib, with no sorry.

The proof is deliberately value-free: it never evaluates ζ, never calls an interval–Newton solver, and never uses continuity or the intermediate value theorem. It rests on three ingredients. (i) Antitonicity: the real Dirichlet series ζ(p) = Σn≥1 n−p, together with the two budget functions, is proved strictly decreasing on (1, ∞) — a fact Mathlib does not provide for riemannZeta, with which it is shown to agree on the real axis above 1. (ii) Certified rational brackets: exact integer partial sums, generated by a verified floor oracle, are combined with Mathlib’s integral tail bound to enclose each crossing in an interval with rational endpoints. (iii) A parity separation: the negative budget is strictly below the positive one, term by term, because every odd denominator exceeds the corresponding even one. The ordering then follows from a three-way case analysis on real trichotomy; the entire numeric input is a handful of integer inequalities discharged by native_decide, decide and norm_num.

As a by-product the argument is level-independent: the same proof yields pN(L) < pP for every level L ≥ 1, which covers both the normalisation of the source document (L = 1, pN = 1.3777851698375411838) and the one stated in the formal objective (L = 2, pN = 1.2136006221625785622).

1. Introduction and context

The rh42 round studies a signed greedy expansion. A real target x is approximated by a series of signed terms whose magnitudes are the powers n−p, with p > 1 a fixed exponent (the “helix parameter”). The terms are indexed by parity: even indices are the positive side, odd indices the negative side. Two quantities control what the rule can possibly reach.

The two budgets
P(p) = ∑k≥1 (2k)−p = 2−p ζ(p)    (positive budget)
N(p) = ∑k≥1 (2k+1)−p = ∑k≥0 (2k+3)−p    (negative budget)

and the two identities that organise them (source §5.1, ledger rows T2–T3):

P(p) + N(p) = ζ(p) − 1,     P(p) − N(p) = 1 − η(p),     η(p) = (1 − 21−p) ζ(p).

The sign of the second identity is the structural point: η(p) is an alternating series with value strictly between 1 − 2−p and 1, so P − N > 0 always. The positive side is the cheaper one. Everything in this paper is a consequence of that asymmetry, made quantitative.

1.1 The three thresholds

Three levels of the budgets carry interpretation (§5.3): the positive budget reaching 1 means the target sin = +1 becomes writable; the negative budget reaching its level means sin = −1 becomes writable; and the combined budget P + N = ζ − 1 reaching 1 is the threshold of the earlier round rh41.

Two conventions matter and are stated plainly, because the formal statement and the source document differ in one respect that the reader should not have to discover.

A normalisation to keep straight

The source writes the negative equation as (1 − 2−p)ζ(p) = 2, which in terms of the budget is N(p) = 1 — Appendix A of the source indeed annotates pN as “N(p) = 1”. The formal objective for this development instead fixes the negative budget at the level 2. These are two different points on the same strictly decreasing function: level 1 at p = 1.3777851698375411838, level 2 at p = 1.2136006221625785622.

The file proves the ordering for level 2 (tpp_rh42_three_thresholds) and for an arbitrary level L ≥ 1 (tpp_rh42_three_thresholds_level), so the source’s own normalisation L = 1 is covered as an instance. The values themselves are not formalised; see §13.

2. Preliminaries

2.1 Notation and the three functions

Throughout, p, q, L are real, k, n, N are natural, and ∑' denotes Mathlib’s unconditional sum (tsum), which equals the limit of the partial sums whenever the family is summable.

noncomputable def ZetaR (p : ℝ) : ℝ := ∑' n : ℕ, ((n : ℝ) ^ p)⁻¹

noncomputable def BPos (p : ℝ) : ℝ := (2 : ℝ) ^ (-p) * ZetaR p

noncomputable def BNeg (p : ℝ) : ℝ := ∑' k : ℕ, ((2 * (k : ℝ) + 3) ^ p)⁻¹

So ZetaR p is the real Dirichlet series Σn≥1 n−p (the n = 0 term vanishes for p > 0, since 0p = 0), BPos p is the positive budget P, and BNeg p is the negative budget N written as the odd series starting at 3. Writing N as a series rather than as the closed form (1 − 2−p)ζ(p) − 1 is the central design decision of the development; §6 explains why it makes the separation N < P immediate.

2.2 Why not riemannZeta?

Mathlib provides riemannZeta : ℂ → ℂ. It is the wrong tool here for three reasons, all of which are visible in the type alone:

  • it is complex-valued, so it has no order structure to exploit;
  • it carries no monotonicity statement on the real interval (1, ∞);
  • it carries no numeric bounds: nothing in Mathlib turns riemannZeta into a rational enclosure, and its definitional unfolding goes through the completed zeta function and the functional equation rather than the Dirichlet series.

The function one actually wants is the Dirichlet series itself, and that is what ZetaR is. These three objections are about usability, not about identity: on the real axis above 1 the two functions agree, and that bridge is proved in the development (§2.5). What no step of the ordering proof needs is the complex object.

2.3 Summability and the shift identity

Lemma 2.1 — summability

For p > 1:  summable_zetaR gives that n ↦ (np)−1 is summable (from Real.summable_nat_rpow_inv, Mathlib’s p-series criterion); summable_shift reindexes it to k ↦ ((k+1)p)−1; and summable_BNeg dominates k ↦ ((2k+3)p)−1 by the shifted series, using k+1 ≤ 2k+3 and Summable.of_nonneg_of_le.

Lemma 2.2 — shift
zetaR_shift (hp : 1 < p) : ZetaR p = ∑' k, (((k : ℝ) + 1) ^ p)⁻¹
zetaR_eq_shift_neg (hp : 1 < p) : ZetaR p = ∑' k, (((k + 1 : ℕ)) : ℝ) ^ (-p)

Proof: move the n = 0 term out with Summable.tsum_eq_zero_add; it vanishes because 0p = 0 for p > 0. The second form is the one used everywhere below, because the exponent −p is the shape in which Real.rpow_neg applies.

2.4 Strict antitonicity of the zeta series

Lemma 2.3 — ZetaR_strictAnti

If 1 < p and p < q then ζ(q) < ζ(p).

This is the fact Mathlib does not have, and it is where the “value-free” claim is earned: from it, every budget function is monotone, and monotonicity alone suffices to compare crossings once rational brackets are known. The proof is a termwise comparison of two convergent series.

Write c(n) = n−p − n−q. Because n ↦ n−x is decreasing in x for n > 1, every c(n) is nonnegative, and c(0) = 0. The n = 2 term is strictly positive, since 2−q < 2−p for p < q. Hence the difference series has a strictly positive sum, by Summable.le_tsum applied at index 2. Since both series are summable, Summable.tsum_sub identifies the difference of the sums with the sum of the differences, and ζ(p) − ζ(q) > 0.

Why the strictness matters

Every crossing comparison in §7 needs strict antitonicity. A one-line non-strict version would not do: the whole content of T14 is that the three crossings are ordered, and at p* the two budgets sum to exactly 1, so the inequalities are tight at the endpoints. The proof above buys strictness from the single term n = 2.

2.5 The bridge to Mathlib’s riemannZeta

The development works with the Dirichlet series, but it does not leave the classical ζ disconnected: the two agree on the real axis above 1, and the agreement is proved rather than assumed.

Lemma 2.4 — ZetaR_eq_riemannZeta_re

For every real p > 1,

ZetaR p = (riemannZeta (p : ℂ)).re

that is, the real Dirichlet series is the real part of Mathlib’s riemannZeta at the real point p.

Proof. zeta_eq_tsum_one_div_nat_cpow states that riemannZeta s = Σ' n, 1 / (n : ℂ) ^ s whenever 1 < s.re. Each summand is the coercion of a real one: (n : ℂ)p = ↑((n : ℝ)p) by Complex.ofReal_cpow together with Complex.ofReal_natCast, and 1 / ↑x = ↑(x−1) by Complex.ofReal_inv. So the complex series is exactly ↑(Σ' n, ((n : ℝ)p)−1), by Complex.ofReal_tsum — which is the statement that the coercion commutes with the infinite sum — and Complex.ofReal_re reads off the real part, leaving the definition of ZetaR.

Corollary 2.5 — the budgets in terms of riemannZeta
BPos_eq_riemannZeta_re : BPos p = 2 ^ (-p) * (riemannZeta (p : ℂ)).re
Nside_eq_riemannZeta_re : Nside p = (1 - 2 ^ (-p)) * (riemannZeta (p : ℂ)).re

so both budgets of the source’s §5.1 are expressed against classical ζ and not merely against the series.

The factor 2−p is not decoration

It is tempting to read the bridge as “P(p) is the real part of ζ”. That is false. P sums over even denominators only, so P(p) = 2−p Re ζ(p), whereas Re ζ(p) is ZetaR itself. At p = 2 the two differ by a factor of 4: P(2) = π2/24 against Re ζ(2) = π2/6. The development records the no-go form explicitly,

BPos_ne_riemannZeta_re : BPos 2 ≠ (riemannZeta (2 : ℂ)).re

proved without evaluating ζ(2) at all: if the two agreed then Re ζ(2) = (1/4) Re ζ(2), hence Re ζ(2) = 0, contradicting ZetaR_pos. Recording a false form next to its true neighbour is deliberate: it is the precise mistake the notation invites, and it costs one lemma to make it impossible to repeat.

2.6 The shape of the whole argument

Three ingredients, each proved independently:

  1. Monotonicity. ZetaR_strictAnti, BPos_strictAnti, BNeg_strictAnti — all three budgets strictly decrease on (1, ∞).
  2. Brackets. ZetaR_4_3_gt, ZetaR_3_2_gt, ZetaR_3_2_lt, ZetaR_7_5_lt — four rational enclosures of ζ at rational arguments, built from exact integer arithmetic.
  3. Separation. BNeg_lt_BPos — N(p) < P(p) for p > 1, term by term.

Their product is the ordering. Continuity, the intermediate value theorem, and any computation of an actual root are all absent from the final assembly, and §7 explains why they can be: the crossing points enter the theorem as hypotheses (they are defined by their equations), so the theorem is a statement about the relative position of the solutions, not about their existence.

3. The abstract ordering core

The module TppLean/Core.lean isolates the order-theoretic content of T14 and proves it for arbitrary real functions, so that nothing in it depends on zeta, on parity, or on any number.

structure CrossesOnce (B : ℝ → ℝ) (q L : ℝ) : Prop where
  cont      : ContinuousOn B (Set.Ici q)
  anti      : ∀ x y, q < x → q < y → x < y → B y < B x
  above_q   : L < B q
  below_far : ∃ x, q < x ∧ B x < L

A budget that is continuous, strictly decreasing, above its level at the left endpoint, and below it somewhere to the right crosses that level exactly once. Existence is the intermediate value theorem; uniqueness is antitonicity.

Theorem 3.1 — CrossesOnce.exists_unique

If CrossesOnce B q L then there is exactly one c > q with B(c) = L.

Proof. Apply intermediate_value_Icc' on [q, x] to L ∈ [B(x), B(q)], obtaining c with B(c) = L; the endpoint c = q is excluded by above_q. Uniqueness is a real trichotomy: a second solution d on either side of c would violate anti, giving L < L.

The hypothesis package for the two concrete budgets, and the ordering theorem itself:

structure BudgetHyp (F G : ℝ → ℝ) (q : ℝ) : Prop where
  crossesP : CrossesOnce F q 1
  crossesN : CrossesOnce G q 2
  hGF      : ∀ x, q < x → G x < F x

theorem threshold_ordering {F G : ℝ → ℝ} {q pF pG : ℝ}
    (h : BudgetHyp F G q) (hF : IsThreshold F q 1 pF) (hG : IsThreshold G q 2 pG) :
    pG < pF

The proof is a two-line contradiction. Suppose pF ≤ pG. Since G < F everywhere and F(pF) = 1, we get G(pF) < 1. But if pF < pG then antitonicity gives G(pF) > G(pG) = 2, and if pF = pG then G(pF) = 2. Either way G(pF) is both below 1 and at least 2.

The moral of the core

The ordering of two crossings is order theory, not analysis. Analysis is needed only to know that the crossings exist and are unique — and even that is not needed to state T14, because T14 is a statement about solutions that are given by their defining equations. This is the observation that lets §7 avoid the intermediate value theorem entirely: the core is available and fully proved, but the concrete theorem is assembled from monotonicity alone, which is why the concrete development needs no continuity of ζ — something Mathlib would make expensive.

4. Certified rational arithmetic: the floor oracle

Ingredient (2) is the interesting engineering. To bracket ζ(r) for a rational r = num/den we use partial sums, and we want the partial sums to be computed by the kernel rather than asserted. The obstacle is that 109/knum/den is irrational for the exponents we need, so the summands cannot simply be natural numbers.

The resolution: compute exact floors at scale 109, and absorb the floor error into the tail term.

4.1 The search and its invariant

def SCn : ℕ := 10 ^ 9

def go (den knum : ℕ) : ℕ → ℕ → ℕ → ℕ
  | 0, lo, _ => lo
  | f + 1, lo, hi =>
      if lo + 1 < hi then
        let mid := (lo + hi) / 2
        if mid ^ den * knum ≤ SCn ^ den then go den knum f mid hi
        else go den knum f lo mid
      else lo

go is a binary search for the largest j with jden · knum ≤ SCnden. Its correctness is a single induction on the fuel, with three invariants carried along:

lemma go_spec (den knum : ℕ) :
    ∀ (f lo hi : ℕ), lo ≤ hi → lo ^ den * knum ≤ SCn ^ den →
      (∀ j, hi ≤ j → SCn ^ den < j ^ den * knum) → hi - lo ≤ 2 ^ f →
      (go den knum f lo hi) ^ den * knum ≤ SCn ^ den ∧
      (∀ j, go den knum f lo hi < j → SCn ^ den < j ^ den * knum)
  1. Admissibility of lo. Preserved trivially in both branches.
  2. Inadmissibility above hi. When the midpoint is rejected, monotonicity of j ↦ jden · knum (Nat.pow_le_pow_left) propagates the rejection upward, so hi can be moved down to the midpoint.
  3. Fuel sufficiency. The width halves each round, and the two sub-intervals are each at most half of hi - lo; omega discharges the arithmetic. With SCn + 1 ≤ 264 as the starting width, 64 rounds suffice.
The oracle 
def fpow (num den k : ℕ) : ℕ :=
  if k = 0 then SCn else go den (k ^ num) 64 0 (SCn + 1)

Then fpow num den k is exactly the floor ⌊109 / knum/den⌋, and four lemmas say so in the two directions needed:

fpow_valid : fpow num den k ^ den * k ^ num ≤ SCn ^ den
fpow_max   : fpow num den k < n → n ≤ SCn + 1 → SCn ^ den < n ^ den * k ^ num
fpow_le    : (fpow num den k : ℝ) ≤ SCn / (k : ℝ) ^ ((num : ℝ) / den)
le_fpow    : SCn / (k : ℝ) ^ ((num : ℝ) / den) < (fpow num den k : ℝ) + 1

The step from the integer statement fpow_valid to the real statement fpow_le is where the irrational exponent is handled: raise both sides to the den-th power, use (knum/den)den = knum (rpow_div_pow, from Real.rpow_mul), and take den-th roots with pow_le_pow_iff_left₀.

4.2 The bridges: from integer sums to ζ

def sumF (num den N : ℕ) : ℕ := ∑ k ∈ Finset.range N, fpow num den (k + 1)

sumF is the certified partial sum, an integer. Two lemmas convert between it and the real partial sum Σk<N (k+1)−num/den:

Lemmas 4.1 and 4.2 — the bridges
sumF_le_zetaR_partial : (sumF num den N : ℝ) ≤ SCn * ∑ k ∈ range N, ((k+1 : ℕ) : ℝ) ^ (-(num/den))
zetaR_partial_le : SCn * ∑ k ∈ range N, ((k+1 : ℕ) : ℝ) ^ (-(num/den)) ≤ (sumF num den N : ℝ) + N

Read together: the certified integer sum and 109 times the true partial sum differ by at most N. Indeed each term satisfies fpow ≤ 109k−num/den < fpow+1 by fpow_le and le_fpow, and summing N such one-sided errors accumulates to N on one side and zero on the other.

Why this is the right trade

The floor error is asymmetric by construction: the lower bracket is exact, the upper bracket is too large by at most N/109. This is exactly the slack structure the application needs — lower bounds on ζ are used to prove P(4/3) > 1, upper bounds to prove P(3/2) < 1 — and the N/109 term is then comfortably smaller than the analytic tail that follows it. Choosing N = 104 makes the slack 10−5 against a tail of 2 × 10−2: two orders of magnitude of room.

4.3 The tail

The partial sums are closed by Mathlib’s integral test. The development contains its own statement of the comparison in the exact indexed form needed:

Lemma 4.3 — tail_finite, tsum_tail_le

For p > 1, N ≥ 1 and every truncation length M:

∑i<M (N+i+1)−p  ≤  N1−pp−1

and passing to the limit (HasSum.tendsto_sum_nat against a constant sequence) the same bound holds for the infinite tail Σk (k+N+1)−p.

The finite statement is AntitoneOn.sum_le_integral_Ico applied to x ↦ x−p on each interval of the partition, then intervalIntegral.integral_rpow evaluates the integral to (N1−p − (N+M)1−p)/(p−1), which is bounded by dropping the negative term.

With the split identity ZetaR_split (ζ(p) = Σk<N(k+1)−p + tail) and the two bridges, we obtain the two usable forms, which are the only statements about ζ that the rest of the development consumes:

Lemmas 4.4 and 4.5 — the working brackets
ZetaR_lower : (sumF num den N : ℝ) / SCn ≤ ZetaR p   for p = num/den > 1, Nnum ≤ SCnden
ZetaR_upper : ZetaR p ≤ ((sumF num den N : ℝ) + N) / SCn + N ^ (1 - p) / (p - 1)

5.3 From brackets to budget brackets

Four more lemmas turn the ζ-enclosures into statements about the budgets themselves. They are pure multiplication of inequalities with positivity side conditions, e.g.

P(4/3) = 2−4/3 ζ(4/3)  >  (1/2.52) · 3.42  >  1,

using 2−4/3 > 1/2.52 from 24/3 < 2.52 (two_rpow_neg_4_3_gt), and

P(3/2) = 2−3/2 ζ(3/2)  <  (1/2.82) · 2.64  <  1 .

The results are BPos_4_3_gt_one : 1 < BPos (4/3) and BPos_3_2_lt_one : BPos (3/2) < 1. Together with antitonicity of P these pin pP to the interval (4/3, 3/2).

6. Gap (1): the parity separation

This is the step the source describes as “the positive side is always the cheaper one” (row T4), and it is the one gap that is genuinely about the two budgets rather than about ζ alone.

The positive budget is the even-index sub-series. Writing it that way is the key move:

Lemma 6.1 — BPos_eq_even
P(p) = 2−p ζ(p) = ∑k≥0 (2k+2)−p

Proof. Shift: ζ(p) = Σk(k+1)−p (zetaR_eq_shift_neg). Then 2−p(k+1)−p = (2(k+1))−p by Real.mul_rpow, and the factor 2−p is pulled through the sum by tsum_mul_left.

Now the negative and positive budgets are sums over the same index set, with denominators 2k+3 and 2k+2 respectively. The comparison is termwise:

Lemma 6.2 — odd_lt_even

For p > 1 and every k:

(2k+3)−p  <  (2k+2)−p

Proof. The map x ↦ xp is strictly increasing on the positives, so (2k+2)p < (2k+3)p (Real.rpow_lt_rpow). Inverting a strictly positive inequality reverses it (one_div_lt_one_div_of_lt), and x−p = (xp)−1 is Real.rpow_neg.

Theorem 6.3 — BNeg_lt_BPos (gap 1)

For every p > 1:   N(p)  <  P(p).

Proof. Rewrite P by Lemma 6.1, then apply Summable.tsum_lt_tsum with the pointwise bound (le_of_lt of Lemma 6.2, valid at every k) and the strict witness at k = 0. Both series are summable: summable_BNeg and summable_even.

Why the series form is the right definition

The source proves P − N = 1 − η(p) and then η(p) < 1. That route requires the alternating-series identity for η and the evaluation of the even/odd rearrangement of ζ. By defining N as its series rather than as (1 − 2−p)ζ(p) − 1, the same content drops out of tsum_lt_tsum with no rearrangement at all. This is the single design decision that made gap (1) short.

The companion strict-antitonicity of the negative budget is the same termwise argument with the exponent playing the role of the base: for p < q, (2k+3)−q < (2k+3)−p because the base is larger than 1 (Real.rpow_lt_rpow_of_exponent_lt), giving BNeg_strictAnti. The positive budget, being a product of two strictly decreasing positive factors, gives BPos_strictAnti.

7. The main theorem

Theorem 7.1 — tpp_rh42_three_thresholds 
theorem tpp_rh42_three_thresholds (pP pN pstar : ℝ)
    (hP : BPos pP = 1) (hN : BNeg pN = 2) (hstar : ZetaR pstar = 2) :
    pN < pP ∧ pP < pstar
Theorem 7.2 — tpp_rh42_three_thresholds_level 
theorem tpp_rh42_three_thresholds_level (pP pN pstar L : ℝ) (hL : 1 ≤ L)
    (hP : BPos pP = 1) (hN : BNeg pN = L) (hstar : ZetaR pstar = 2) :
    pN < pP ∧ pP < pstar

Theorem 7.2 is not a separate argument: it is the proof of Theorem 7.1 with the single hypothesis hL : 1 ≤ L added. The point of stating it is that the source’s own normalisation is L = 1, so the source’s claim is an instance.

7.1 The five moves

Everything below is monotonicity applied to the three proved antitonicity lemmas, with the brackets of §5 supplying the numeric anchors.

Move 1 — 1 < pP (one_lt_of_BPos_eq)

If pP ≤ 1 we want a contradiction from P(pP) = 1. The point is that the Dirichlet series does not converge there: by Real.summable_nat_rpow_inv, summability of n ↦ (np)−1 is equivalent to p > 1, so for p ≤ 1 the series is not summable and tsum_eq_zero_of_not_summable gives ζ(p) = 0. Hence P(p) = 2−p · 0 = 0 ≠ 1. The same argument for the level-2 equation gives one_lt_of_zetaR_eq.

Move 2 — 4/3 < pP

Trichotomy on (4/3, pP). The increasing case is the goal; the equal case contradicts BPos_4_3_gt_one against hP; the decreasing case pP < 4/3 uses BPos_strictAnti (legitimate because Move 1 supplies pP > 1) to get P(4/3) < P(pP) = 1, contradicting P(4/3) > 1.

Move 3 — BNeg pP < 1

Apply gap (1) at pP (again legitimate by Move 1) and substitute hP:

N(pP)  <  P(pP)  =  1 .

This one line is the whole point of gap (1), and it is the only place the parity separation is used.

Move 4 — pN < pP

Trichotomy on (pN, pP). The increasing case is the goal. If pN = pP then N(pP) = L, which together with Move 3 and L ≥ 1 reads L ≤ 1 > L. If instead pP < pN then BNeg_strictAnti gives N(pN) < N(pP), i.e. L < 1 ≤ L. Notice that this move needs no lower bound on pN whatsoever: the antitonicity is applied with pP > 1 as the left endpoint, which is why Move 1 was about pP and not about pN.

Move 5 — pP < 3/2 < p*

Two independent applications of the same pattern. For pP: trichotomy on (pP, 3/2) with BPos_3_2_lt_one, i.e. P(3/2) < 1 = P(pP). For p*: first one_lt_of_zetaR_eq gives p* > 1, then trichotomy on (3/2, p*) with ZetaR_3_2_gt, i.e. 2 < ζ(3/2) = 2.

Chaining:

pN  <  pP  <  32  <  p* .
Where did the analysis go?

The proof uses analytic input in exactly two places, both inside lemmas proved once and reused: strict antitonicity of the series (§2.4) and the integral tail bound (§4.3). The final assembly is nineteen lines of real trichotomy. In particular it never needs to know that ζ is continuous, never invokes the intermediate value theorem, never locates a root numerically, and never evaluates ζ. That is what makes the formalisation tractable: continuity of a Dirichlet series on (1, ∞) is a uniform-convergence argument that Mathlib would make expensive, whereas antitonicity is a termwise inequality.

8.3 What is not in the file

Honest scope
  • The numerical values are not formalised. The file proves the ordering; it does not prove pP = 1.4649845878051603837. Locating the roots to 19 digits is the source’s 80-dps computation, and formalising it would need a verified root-finder (see §13).
  • The budget identities are not formalised. The source’s P + N = ζ − 1 and P − N = 1 − η (ledger rows T2–T3) do not appear as theorems. The separation they would be used for is proved directly instead (§6), so nothing depends on them — but the reviewer should know that the file does not establish the even/odd rearrangement of ζ, only the inequality it implies.
  • BPos p is not itself the real part of riemannZeta p — the factor 2−p is real. The bridge itself is now proved: ZetaR p = (riemannZeta (p : ℂ)).re (ZetaR_eq_riemannZeta_re, §2.5), whence BPos p = 2^(-p) * (riemannZeta p).re and Nside p = (1 - 2^(-p)) * (riemannZeta p).re. The literal form would differ by a factor of 4 at p = 2 and is recorded as false (BPos_ne_riemannZeta_re).
  • The abstract core is not on the critical path. Core.threshold_ordering is proved and available, but the concrete theorem does not call it: the concrete assembly uses trichotomy directly, which is the same argument with continuity removed. This is a redundancy, not a gap.

9. Conclusion

T14 is now a theorem rather than a numerical observation. Concretely, for ζ(p) = Σn≥1n−p, P(p) = 2−pζ(p) and N(p) = Σk≥0(2k+3)−p, if

P(pP) = 1,    N(pN) = L  (L ≥ 1),    ζ(p*) = 2,

then pN < pP < p*. The proof is value-free, carries no sorry, and every numeric step is an integer or rational inequality checked by the kernel (with the three large sums checked by the compiler).

The shape of the argument is worth restating because it is transferable: to order the solutions of several monotone equations, you do not need to solve them, and you do not need continuity. You need strict monotonicity of each function, one evaluated point per equation to anchor the comparison, and one inequality separating the functions from each other. Here the separating inequality is the parity comparison N < P, and it does the entire job of placing pN before pP.

10. Significance

10.1 For the ledger

The rh42 round keeps a ledger with an explicit entry rule: VERIFIED requires recomputation by an independent route, PROVED requires a written argument. T14 was VERIFIED only, with the caveat that root-finding at 80 digits is evidence about a computation, not a proof about a limit. This development moves the row to PROVED and machine-checked, and it does so without re-running the numerics: the proof is of the same order as the claim.

10.2 For the budgets method

The budgets P and N are the reachable ranges of the signed greedy rule; the thresholds are where those ranges cease to cover the targets. The ordering pN < pP is the quantitative form of the statement that the negative side becomes unable to express −1 strictly earlier than the positive side becomes unable to express +1 — the “half-turn deficit” of the alternating series. Making that asymmetry a theorem, rather than an estimate, is what lets the downstream reachable-set analysis (rows T10–T13) cite it.

10.3 For formalisation practice

Mathlib has no monotonicity and no numeric bounds for riemannZeta. This development shows that both can be supplied cheaply for the real Dirichlet series, that the two functions are provably the same object on the real axis above 1 (Lemma 2.4), and that the combination is enough for a concrete ordering result. The two reusable artefacts are ZetaR_strictAnti (a strict-antitonicity result obtained from one strictly positive term of a difference series, a pattern that transfers to any real Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients) and the floor-oracle-plus-bridge architecture of §4 (a way to turn irrational-exponent series computations into kernel-checkable integer arithmetic with a controlled error budget).

10.4 For the interpretation

At p* the budget interval has length exactly ζ(p*) − 1 = 1 but is split unevenly, so the endpoints ±1 are still not reachable there. T14 is precisely the statement that makes this non-vacuous: the two endpoint thresholds are strictly inside (1, p*), so the failure at p* is a genuine failure of placement, not of length.

11. Novelty

Four things in this development are, as far as the session establishes, not standard practice.

  1. Ordering without continuity or root-finding. The usual way to compare crossings of monotone functions is to locate both roots numerically and compare the enclosures. Here the roots are hypotheses, and only the relative position is proved, so the analytic input reduces to strict antitonicity. This is not merely an aesthetic simplification: it removes the intermediate value theorem and the continuity of a Dirichlet series from the dependency cone entirely.
  2. A verified floor oracle for irrational exponents. fpow computes ⌊109/knum/den⌋ by a fuel-recursive binary search whose correctness is a single induction with three invariants (go_spec). This replaces the usual approach of bounding knum/den by pointwise integer surrogates, which in this problem provably loses too much: the pointwise surrogates available for ζ(3/2) < 2.64 sum to roughly 1.0–1.2 against a true value of 2.59, whereas the exact floors sum to 2.5924.
  3. The two-sided bridge with asymmetric error. sumF_le_zetaR_partial and zetaR_partial_le sandwich 109 times the true partial sum between the integer sum and the integer sum plus N. The asymmetry (exact below, N above) is deliberate and is what the application consumes: lower bounds are used where an exactness is needed, upper bounds where an analytic tail dominates the slack by two orders of magnitude.
  4. The parity separation as a termwise series inequality. The source proves P > N via the alternating series η and the rearrangement P + N = ζ − 1. Defining N by its series makes the comparison termwise and the strictness free (witness k = 0), which is what made gap (1) the short one.

12. Applications

12.1 Direct use inside the rh42 programme

  • Rows T10–T13. The completeness criteria for the positive and negative halves (thresholds p* and q = 2.07677517921069) and the reachable-set descriptions at p = 2, 3 all need the threshold ordering as an input. T14 gives it in a form that can be cited rather than recomputed.
  • The budget interval at p* (row T15). The claim that the interval has length 1 but excludes ±1 depends on knowing that pP, pN < p*.
  • Reachability arguments that must not be circular. Any argument of the form “the target is already out of budget here, because the budget crossed below it earlier” needs a strict ordering of crossings; T14 supplies exactly that.

12.2 Transferable technique

  • Certified enclosures for special functions. The pattern integer partial sum + analytic tail + rational bridge is the general recipe for turning a series definition into a rigorous enclosure. It applies to any Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients, to polylogarithms on the real axis, and to η, β, λ and friends.
  • Verified numeric constants for other ledgers. Any claim of the form “this threshold lies in that interval” can be discharged by the same oracle plus the same tail bound, at whatever precision the scale constant SCn = 109 is set to. Raising SCn costs only a longer fuel bound and larger integers.
  • Complete-sequence criteria. Brown’s criterion — the completeness of a subset-sum set generated by a decreasing sequence — is the abstract form of the budget test, and its hypotheses are of exactly the antitonicity-plus-anchor shape proved here. The file’s separation and bracket lemmas are the two inputs such a criterion consumes.

12.3 Beyond

Outside number theory, the argument is an instance of a common situation: several quantities defined implicitly by monotone equations, whose relative order is what matters and whose values are not needed. Certificate-holding systems, resource budgets, and any setting where one compares activation thresholds of decreasing response curves have the same shape, and the proof strategy — monotonicity plus one anchor per curve plus one separating inequality — transfers verbatim.

13. Further research

13.1 Inside this development

  1. Formalise the numerical values. The file proves the ordering but not pP = 1.4649845878051603837. A verified interval-Newton or interval-bisection solver on the three equations, reusing ZetaR_lower and ZetaR_upper as the enclosure engine, would close this. The ingredients are present; what is missing is a rational interval arithmetic layer and a contraction argument.
  2. Formalise the budget identities. P + N = ζ − 1 and P − N = 1 − η are ledger rows T2–T3. The obstruction encountered here was the even/odd rearrangement Σ' k, f (2k) + Σ' k, f (2k+1) = Σ' k, f k via tsum_even_add_odd, which is available in Mathlib and eluded the elaboration attempts made in this session; a working instance would also give P − N = 1 − η and hence an independent second proof of gap (1).
  3. Formalise continuity, then use the abstract core. Core.threshold_ordering is proved but unused. Proving ContinuousOn (ZetaR) on (1, ∞) — locally uniform convergence of the Dirichlet series — would let the concrete instantiation feed the abstract core, unifying the two routes and yielding existence of the thresholds as well as their order.
  4. Push the level generalisation further. Theorem 7.2 covers L ≥ 1. The case L < 1 requires a different anchor, since N(pP) < 1 is no longer enough. The natural anchor would be a bracket for N itself, which is the odd-series analogue of tail_finite and is not in the file.

13.2 Adjacent claims in the same ledger

  1. T13 — the negative threshold q = 2.07677517921069. Defined by N(q) = 2 · 3−q. The right-hand side is the first odd term, so this is a self-referential budget condition; the same monotonicity plus bracket strategy should apply, with the extra step of showing the two sides cross once.
  2. T10 — Brown’s criterion. “The positive half is complete iff ζ(p) ≥ 2” is the conceptual parent of T14. Formalising Brown’s criterion for real sequences would give the positivity/negativity half of the reachable-set theory, and the two-sided version would give the negative half.
  3. T11–T12 — the reachable set has gaps. At p = 2 the subset-sum set is two intervals; at p = 3 it is four. These are finite combinatorial statements about a greedy rule, plausibly the most tractable remaining rows, and they do not need any zeta input beyond the budgets.

13.3 Methodological

  1. Replace native_decide with decide. The three large sum evaluations currently rest on the compiler. A kernel-only route — for instance a verified Horner-style evaluation of go with a proof-producing certificate, or simply a longer decide — would remove the last trust assumption beyond the kernel.
  2. Generalise the oracle. fpow is specialised to ⌊109/knum/den⌋. A version parameterised by the scale and returning a proof-carrying floor would be a reusable Mathlib candidate, as would ZetaR_strictAnti itself.

14. Adjacent concepts

14.1 The Dirichlet eta function and the alternating series

η(p) = Σn≥1(−1)n−1n−p = (1 − 21−p)ζ(p) is the alternating companion of ζ. It is the reason the positive side is cheaper: the signed sum P − N is the alternating series from n = 2 on, so its positivity is Abel summation on a monotone decreasing sequence. The development replaces that argument with an explicit termwise comparison, but the mechanism is the same. The value η(1) = ln 2 is the boundary case and is where the source’s “alphabet total” row T17 lives.

14.2 The pole at p = 1

As p ↓ 1, ζ(p) ~ 1/(p−1) + γ, and the source observes that the residue is split evenly: P(p) ~ N(p) ~ 1/(2(p−1)). This is the reason “every sine is writable” at p = 1 and the reason the whole problem is confined to p > 1. It is also why Move 1 of §7.1 works the way it does: below and at p = 1 the series is not summable at all, and the formalisation records that as “ζ(p) = 0 by the tsum-of-a-non-summable-family convention” rather than as a limiting statement.

14.3 Complete sequences and Brown’s criterion

A sequence (an) of positive reals is complete if every number in [0, Σan] is a subsum. Brown’s criterion gives a sufficient condition in terms of the growth of the partial-maximum function; the greedy algorithm achieves completeness exactly when the criterion holds. The positive half of the signed rule is the classical Egyptian greedy at 2px (row T8), so the completeness threshold p* is a Brown-type threshold. T14 sits underneath all of this: it is the statement that decides which of the two endpoint targets runs out first.

14.4 Integral comparison and Euler–Maclaurin

The tail bound of §4.3 is the first-order integral test: Σk>Nk−p ≤ N1−p/(p−1). The next term of the Euler–Maclaurin expansion — the −½N−p correction — would tighten every upper bracket by about 5 × 10−3 at N = 104, and would matter if one wanted tighter enclosures or smaller truncation lengths. For the present result the crude bound already leaves two orders of magnitude of slack.

14.5 Interval arithmetic and verified root-finding

The natural next layer is interval arithmetic: represent a real by a pair of rationals, evaluate ζ by the same partial-sum-plus-tail engine, and run interval Newton or bisection until the enclosure is smaller than a target width. This is exactly the machinery needed for §13.1(1), and it is the standard route from “the ordering is proved” to “the values are proved”. The present development can be read as the arithmetic core of such a solver, with the solver itself omitted because the ordering does not need it.

14.6 Related zeta thresholds and the wider programme

The same three-model pattern — a decreasing total budget, an even/odd split, a threshold where a target becomes unreachable — recurs whenever a signed greedy expansion is indexed by parity. The rh41 threshold ζ(p) = 2, the rh42 negative threshold q, and the “four thresholds enclosed” statement of the round’s truths.md are all instances. The value of formalising T14 first is that it is the ordering on which the others are constraining: any later threshold that must lie between pN and p* inherits a proved frame.
