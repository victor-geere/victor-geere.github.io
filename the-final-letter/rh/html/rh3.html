The circles, before the helix

The nested-circle numeral system in three dimensions — twelve digits on the unit circle, one circle per level, and the depth axis that rh4 collapses.

docs/rh3 · the construction rh4 assumes · figures computed, not sketched — Figure 2 computed live in the browser · 2026-08-04

Abstract. rh4 opens by invoking "the companion construction" and closes by citing it as a companion text. That text was never written down, and every document from rh4 to rh29 stands on a restatement of it four paragraphs long. This document is the companion. It builds the system from the bottom: a circle carrying twelve digits at multiples of π/6 (§2), why twelve and not ten (§3), one circle per level stacked one unit apart along a depth axis with the unit fixed by the first circle's radius (§4), the cumulative half-turns and the torsion that turn the stack into a helix (§5–6), and the collapse of the depth axis that turns the helix into a chain of vectors and the chain into a number (§8). That number is η(s), and rh4's Figure 1 is this document's Figure 2 with the depth axis set to zero. Figure 2 is live: two sliders drive the radial decay σ = Re s and the torsion t = Im s, a purple helix threads the a-vertices and a golden one the b-vertices thirty degrees away, and a third slider morphs the stack continuously into rh4's chain. Nothing here is new mathematics and nothing here is a step towards the hypothesis; it is the picture the rest of the arc argues about, drawn where it actually lives.

Part I · The circle

1 · Why this document exists

rh4's abstract begins "The companion construction reads the Dirichlet eta function as the value of a single infinite numeral," and its closing line reads "Companion text: the nested-circle summary (Parts I–II axioms restated in §1–§4)." Unlike every other cross-reference in the arc, that one is not a link, because there is nothing to link to. The construction exists in the corpus only as rh4 §1 — four paragraphs, written for a reader who had just put the original down.

Twenty-six documents were then built on it. rh5 computed its structure group and its continuum limit; rh6 added an axiom to it; rh10 and rh11 identified the axiom it is missing; rh19 onward turned one corner of it into machine-checked arithmetic. All of them assume a picture the reader has never been shown.

This document shows it, and does one thing rh4 could not. Every figure in the arc is a plane figure, and the construction is not planar: it is a stack of circles along an axis, and rh4's Figure 1 is that stack seen end-on with the axis collapsed. Restoring the axis costs nothing mathematically and explains, in one picture, three things that read as coincidences in the flat drawing — why the gears shrink, why consecutive steps reverse, and why "evaluating the numeral" and "projecting along the depth axis" are the same operation.

Standing disclaimer

Nothing in this document is a step towards the Riemann Hypothesis, and nothing in it is new. It is exposition of a construction, plus a rendering of that construction, plus the arithmetic needed to be sure the rendering is honest. The arc's actual position on the hypothesis is rh28 §7 and rh29; it is not improved by anything here.

2 · The circle, and its twelve digits

Start with one circle of radius 1. On it sit twelve marked points at angular offsets that are integer multiples of π/6, named a through l anticlockwise from the positive real axis. These are the primary digits. A numeral in this system is an infinite string of them, one letter per level.

The circle carries a regular polygon whose arc-length between consecutive vertices is the place value at that level — the twelve vertices are not decoration on a continuum but the sample lattice of the level, which is the reading rh26 §1 arrived at from the far end of the arc, after the refinement question had been settled: "The subdivision is not decoration and not information thrown away: it is the sample lattice."

Six of the twelve pairings matter more than the rest. Each digit has an antipode six steps away, and the alphabet's own name for a's antipode is g. The construction identifies that image with the additive inverse of a and writes it A: the letter g and the symbol A denote the same point, and

(1) a + A = 0,   b + B = 0,   …,   six axes,   g ≡ A,   h ≡ B.

This is the phase-opposite rule, and it is the only piece of digit data the whole arc ever uses. Everything below runs on the single axis {a, A}, with one excursion to {b, B} in §10.

3 · Why twelve

Twelve is not arbitrary and it is not decimal habit. A clock of m stations has exactly the symmetries of ℤ/m, so the divisors of m are the sub-gears available inside a single circle, before any train is built. For m = 12 those are

(2) {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12},   of which the primes are   2   and   3.

Two of them are the ones the construction needs at once. The half-turn — the whole engine of §5 — is the sub-gear 2, six stations of twelve; and 3 is the first odd prime, so a twelve-station clock can name the first two primes as rotations of itself. Every prime from 5 upward is foreign to the clock: representable only through the train of circles, never as a symmetry of one. That observation is rh6's, arrived at long after the fact, and it is the sharpest available answer to "why twelve": the arithmetic beyond {2, 3} is exactly what the levels are for.

The comparison is worth making precisely, because twelve is not the smallest count with that property. Ten gives {1, 2, 5, 10} — a half-turn and the prime 5, but no 3 and no way to trisect the circle. Eight gives {1, 2, 4, 8}: a half-turn and nothing odd at all. Six already gives {1, 2, 3, 6} and owns both the half-turn and the 3-gear, so six would do for §5. What twelve adds is the quarter-turn: it is the smallest count divisible by both 4 and 3, and — the same fact stated differently — the smallest count with six sub-gears at all. Below twelve no clock has more than four.

Nothing below depends on the choice. rh5 §2 proves that all twelve digit strings evaluate to eikπ/6η(s) — one orbit of congruent copies — so the object of interest, the set of strings that evaluate to zero, is the same whichever letter one starts from. The clock fixes a frame, not a fact. §10 draws that.

Part II · The depth axis

4 · The depth axis: one unit per level

A numeral has levels, so the system has one circle per level: circle 1, circle 2, circle 3, and so on without end. Two decisions turn that list into a geometry.

The radii shrink. Circle n has radius rn, a positive decreasing sequence — the place values. The classical choice, and the only one used below, is

(3) rn = n−σ,    σ > 0,

so that r1 = 1 for every σ, r2 = 2−σ, and the decay exponent σ is a single dial. At σ = ½ the first four radii are 1, 0.7071, 0.5774, 0.5.

The circles are separated. They are stacked along a depth axis through their common centre, one unit apart, and the unit is the first circle's radius. Circle n sits at depth

(4) zn = n − 1,    measured in units of r1 = 1.

Two things follow immediately, and both are worth stating because the flat drawing hides them. First, the depth coordinate is the level index: reading the numeral is walking down the axis at unit speed, one place value per step. Second, since r1 = 1 independently of σ, the unit does not move when σ does — the spacing stays fixed while the circles shrink around it, so σ is visible in the figure as a taper rather than as an overall scale.

The word "nested" survives from the plan view, where the circles are literally one inside another. Along the axis they are not nested but stacked, and the nesting order is the depth order. rh5 §4 takes that order seriously enough to read it as membership and recover the von Neumann integers from it; here it is just the level index.

5 · Half-turns, and the phase-opposite rule

Now the engine. The absolute orientation of circle n carries a cumulative extra rotation

(5) αn = (n − 1)π,

one half-turn added per level. So circle 1 is unrotated, circle 2 is rotated by π, circle 3 by 2π, and so on. Combined with the phase-opposite rule of §2 this says something sharp: the letter a, written at every level, does not name the same direction at every level. Written on circle 1 it points along a; written on circle 2 the frame has turned by π, so it points along A; on circle 3 back along a. The constant string

(6) aaaa…   presents, level by level, as   aAaA…

and the alternation is not something the writer put there. It is what one fixed letter looks like in a frame that turns by π per level. This is the construction's central conceit and the reason the arc calls its object a numeral rather than a series.

With torsion off, the consequence is visible and dull: every a-point lies on the a–A diameter, alternating pole to pole as the depth increases, and the thread joining them is a planar zigzag confined to one plane through the axis. rh5 §3 says the same thing in plan view — the discrete helix "only bounces along the a–A diameter." That is a statement about the vertices, and it survives everything below. What Figure 2 draws between them is a different question, answered in §6: with the torsion slider at zero the a-points still alternate pole to pole, but the drawn thread sweeps a half turn per level, because a gear that cannot reverse has no other way to get from a to A.

6 · Torsion, and the helix

The third structure is a continuous, monotonic family of rotations ρn laid on top of the half-turns. The classical choice is

(7) ρn = −t log n,    t ∈ ℝ,

and it is what makes the stack a helix rather than a zigzag. The total lab angle of the a-vertex on circle n is therefore

(8) ϑn = αn + ρn = (n − 1)π − t log n,

and the a-vertex itself is the point of the plane of circle n at radius rn and angle ϑn, at depth n − 1. Joining those points across the levels gives the purple thread of Figure 2.

Because log grows slowly, the increment ρn+1 − ρn = −t log(1 + 1/n) is large at the start and dies away, so the torsion shears the deep end of the stack much less than the shallow end. There is a scale at which the two rotations balance. Treating the level as continuous, the phase velocity is π − t/x, which vanishes at

(9) x* = t/π   (= 4.4992… at t = γ₁ = 14.134725…):

inside x* the torsion overpowers the half-turns and the thread winds backwards; past it the half-turns win and the winding reverses. That is rh5 §3's reading, and in the three-dimensional figure it is the visible kink near the shallow end — the first four or five circles turn one way, everything deeper turns the other.

That reading of (8) takes ϑn literally as a signed quantity, and it is worth saying at once that the construction is not obliged to. A gear turns one way. The clock of §2 stores its digits at angles mod 2π and has no winding memory, so what the word actually demands between station n and station n+1 is a rotation known only up to a whole turn, and a physical gear meets that demand by going forward:

and the picture to hold is a nut on a bolt. Sliding one level deeper advances the nut by one pitch and turns it by whatever that pitch demands; it cannot arrive at the next level having decided, halfway, to unscrew. What the clock word leaves free is the pitch's whole-turn part, since c extra turns per level are invisible mod 2π. Fix it once, for the whole thread:

(10) ϑ̃(x) = (x − 1)·p − t log x,    p = π + cτ,    c = ⌈(t − π)/τ⌉+   whole turns per level.

That is the whole construction — a closed form, no interpolation, no per-segment cases. Its rate is

(11) ϑ̃′(x) = p − t/x,    strictly increasing in x,   and ≥ 0 everywhere by the choice of c,

so the thread never backs off, its rate never jumps, and at the turning ring x* = t/π of (9) the rate is exactly c turns per level. The lift reaches the same station as (8) at every level — the two agree exactly mod 2π — and the difference is c whole turns per level, the carries that rh6 §1 books and promotes into Axiom 9.

One choice here deserves its reason, because the obvious alternative fails. rh6 (2) books the minimum forward turn on each segment separately, which is monotone and lawful but has a defect the three-dimensional figure exposes at once: at t = γ₁ the segments then demand 0.94, 0.59, 0.85, 1.00, 0.09 turns, and that collapse from a full turn to a twelfth of one between the fifth and sixth gears is not a fact about the numeral. It is the residual left over on the first segment that no longer needs a carry — an artefact of choosing the carries one segment at a time. A bolt has no such feature, so the carry is made uniform: the same c at every level. The two lifts differ by whole turns per segment and agree at every station; this one is chosen because it is the one whose rate is monotone. The natural unit for a ledger of turns is the turn itself, so write

(12) δn = ϑ̃(n)/τ    turns,    τ = 2π,

and that is what Figure 2 prints under each of the first ten gears. It is also what the figure draws: the purple thread is (10) itself, not (8), so the thread and the ledger and the spines of §11 are one object seen three ways. At t = γ₁ the pitch is c = 2 whole turns per level plus the half-turn, and the ledger reads 0, 0.94, 2.53, 4.38, 6.38, 8.47, 10.62, 12.82, 15.06, 17.32 τ. Its per-level increments — 0.94, 1.59, 1.85, 2.00, 2.09, 2.15, 2.20, 2.24, 2.26 — rise monotonically towards the pitch p/τ = 2.5, which is (11) read one level at a time: the torsion t/x is a brake that bites hardest at the shallow end and lets go with depth. The reversal of (9) has not been hidden, it has been paid for: x* = 4.4992 is where the brake exactly cancels the half-turn, and the gear there is turning at 2.00 turns per level — the carry rate, neither more nor less. Nothing in the numeral changed; only the lift did.

Two dials, then, and that is the whole parameter set: σ, the radial decay, and t, the torsion. They are the real and imaginary parts of one complex number s = σ + it, which §8 explains.

Part III · The value

8 · The collapse: from stack to chain

So far this is geometry with no number in it. The number comes from one rule: each level contributes one vector, and the vectors are chained tail to head.

The level-n contribution is the displacement from circle n's centre to its a-vertex — radius rn, angle ϑn, read in the plane of the circle. Writing that plane as ℂ and using (5), (7), (8),

(13) vn = rn eiϑn = ei(n−1)π rn eiρn = (−1)n−1 λn rn eiρn,

with λn the retuning factor of §12, set to 1 throughout. The half-turn has become a sign, exactly and only because eiπ = −1 — which is the phase-opposite rule (1) written analytically. The value of the numeral is the sum of the chain:

(14) val(aaaa…) = ∞∑n=1 (−1)n−1 λn rn eiρn,

and with the classical specialisation (3), (7),

(15) rn = n−σ,   ρn = −t log n,   λn ≡ 1   ⟹   val(aaaa…) = ∞∑n=1 (−1)n−1ns = η(s),   s = σ + it.

The two dials of §6 were the two coordinates of s all along: the radial decay is Re s and the torsion is Im s.

The morph

Chaining is a geometric operation on the stack, and the stack ⇄ chain slider performs it continuously. At m = 0 each contribution is drawn from its own circle's centre, which lies on the depth axis at z = n − 1. At m = 1 each is drawn from the head of its predecessor, and the depth axis is flattened to zero. In between, the tail of level n is

(16) (1 − m)·(0, 0, n−1)   +   m·(Pn−1, 0),    Pk = k∑j=1 vj,

exact at both ends and continuous between them. The purple line does not change its meaning as the slider moves — it joins the same a-vertices throughout. At m = 1 it has become the partial-sum path, and the figure has become rh4's Figure 1, reproduced below for comparison.

That is the whole point of restoring the depth axis: evaluation is projection. rh4's chain of gears is this stack with its axis set to zero, and the reason the gears in that figure shrink and the reason the arrows reverse are the two decisions of §4 and §5, seen edge-on.

9 · Why η and not ζ

The sum (15) is the Dirichlet eta function, not the zeta function, and the difference is the half-turns. Split the chain into even and odd levels: the even sub-chain is a copy of the whole un-alternated train scaled by 2−s, and the half-turn flips its sign, so it must be taken out twice — once from the sum and once again as its negative. That is the identity

(17) η(s) = (1 − 21−s) ζ(s).

Zeros of ζ in the strip are therefore zeros of η, and the construction sees them. But the direction that matters here is the other one, because it is what the half-turns buy.

Turn the half-turns off in Figure 2 — set the checkbox — and the chain stops closing and runs away. Without alternation the contributions no longer cancel pairwise, and for σ ≤ 1 the train diverges: at s = ½ + iγ₁ the un-alternated partial sums grow like √N/|1 − s|, which is 7.0704 at N = 104 against a measured 7.0705, and doubles each time N quadruples. With the half-turns on, pairing level 2k−1 against level 2k leaves a residue of size O(|s|·k−σ−1), and Σk−σ−1 converges for every σ > 0.

What the half-turns are for

The alternation is the analytic-continuation device. The plain zeta train exists only for σ > 1; the alternating one exists on all of σ > 0, which is where the critical strip is. Geometrically: the reason the strip can be seen at all is that the frame turns by π per level. This is the one axiom of the construction whose analytic content is both load-bearing and completely understood.

The price is one honest blemish, and the construction must own it. The prefactor in (17) has its own zeros, at sk = 1 + 2πik/log 2. At k = 1 that is t = 9.064720…, and there the chain closes exactly — the snap button is provided — while ζ has modulus 1.3511 and nothing arithmetic vanishes at all. These are gauge closures: artifacts of presenting ζ through half-turns, not properties of ζ. rh4 §3 names them and quarantines them, and the discipline it draws is the one to carry forward — "null helix" and "zero of ζ" are not synonyms, and every claim in the arc is about the closures in 0 < σ < 1 that survive re-presentation.

10 · The golden thread

Figure 2 threads two helices, not one. The amber one joins the b-vertices — the digit adjacent to a, thirty degrees away, on its own antipodal axis {b, B}. It is drawn because of what it is not.

Since every b-vertex is its a-vertex rotated by the same fixed angle,

(18) val(bbbb…) = eiπ/6 η(s),    and generally    val(k-th digit string) = eikπ/6 η(s).

The amber helix is the purple one turned rigidly by thirty degrees, at every σ, at every t, at every depth — orbit the figure and it stays a rigid rotation. The consequence is the one §3 promised: the zero set is digit-independent. A closure is not a property of the letter a; it is the simultaneous collapse of the whole clock frame, all twelve helices returning to the origin together. The twelve digits are a frame, and the vanishing is frame-free.

Which is exactly why Figure 2 hides the amber thread unless asked. Having made the point once, a second thread carries no information the first does not — it is spine 1 where the purple is spine 0, and every claim about one is a claim about all twelve. The b path checkbox restores it, and the honest use of it is to switch it on, orbit, and confirm the rigidity, then switch it off again and read the a-thread against the spines without a second curve crossing it.

It is worth saying why this attractive object goes no further, since a reader meeting the construction here will reach for it. The pairing ⟨a, b⟩ = val(aaaa…)·val(bbbb…) = e−iπ/6|η(s)|² has Re⟨a, b⟩ = cos(π/6)|η(s)|² ≥ 0, with equality exactly at closures: a pairing with a definite sign, vanishing precisely on the zero set. That looks like the missing axiom, and it is not. Its positivity is the free positivity of a norm-square — cos(π/6)|F(s)|² ≥ 0 holds for every Dirichlet series whatsoever, with or without an Euler product, with or without its zeros on a line. rh5 §2 computes this and classifies it: definite sign is cheap pointwise and expensive spectrally. The pairing the arc actually needs is rh4 §15's, and it is still missing at rh29.

11 · The outline is a cone

Switch on the cone in Figure 2. The circles' silhouette, interpolated between the levels, is the surface of revolution

(19) r(x) = x−σ,    depth x − 1,    x ∈ [1, ∞),

drawn with four spines, at a, d, g, j — the object rh5 §3 calls the invisible cone and rh6 sharpens into the horn. Switching it on adds no new curve, because the thread is already one of them. The purple thread is drawn at radius x−σ and the ledger angle of (10), so it lies on r = x−σ at every x and not merely at the integers — and the reversal at x* = t/π of (9) appears on it as the depth where the thread is turning at exactly the carry rate c, not as a turning point.

The spines are not meridians, and the difference is the whole point of §5. A meridian is a fixed direction in the lab; a spine is a digit, and the digits are carried by a frame that turns. So spine k is drawn, in (20), at

(20) angle = ϑ̃(x) + kπ/6,    r = x−σ,    z = x − 1,

where δ is the ledger of (12), and (10) gives it at continuous depth in closed form, so nothing is interpolated: a spine is drawn from the same formula whether it is read at a station or between two. That matters, because a spine is a physical thing and its rate is as visible as its position — the rate here is (11), rising smoothly with depth and never jumping at a station, which is what "nut on a bolt" buys. Because δ only increases, every spine winds one way, the same way the gears do; none of them doubles back at x*, and below the turning ring they merely climb more slowly. The consequence is the one to look for in the figure: the spine that leaves a on circle 1 meets a on every circle, and it gets there having completed exactly the turns the ledger says, no more and no fewer. The four drawn spines are a rigid clock cross screwed onto the cone — a–g is the a–A diameter of §2 and d–j is the one at right angles to it — and with the torsion off they are half-turn-per-level spirals, the more torsion the tighter the screw. All twelve are there in the geometry; four are drawn because twelve close into an opaque skirt, and they are cut at the eighth gear because past it the taper has pulled them into the axis and they say nothing further.

Spine 0 is the a-spine, and the purple thread is drawn on it — identically, not merely at the stations. That is the whole reason the ledger was worth defining: the a-path a reader traces with a finger and the total the gear has turned are then the same statement, and the figure cannot show the numeral advancing while the drawing runs backwards. The turns that (8) would have unwound below x* have not been lost; they are the carries of rh6 §1, and they are printed under the gears. The amber b-thread is spine 1 in exactly the same sense, and is off by default (§10).

The cone is the natural thing to reach for and it is a trap, so the warning belongs here rather than three documents later. Replace the sum over circles by the integral over the cone and the value is a product of Γ-factors and exponentials — and Γ has no zeros. The smeared train never closes: not at the gauge points, not at γ₁, nowhere. Every closure the numeral has is an interference effect of the integer lattice, invisible to the cone. One cannot prove rigidity by smoothing and controlling the difference, because the zeros are the difference. The discreteness of the levels is not a defect the cone smooths away; it is the sole source of everything the hypothesis is about.

12 · Retuning, named and left alone

The seventh structure of the construction is retuning: the radii may be multiplied by continuous factors λn ∈ (0, 1], varying continuously with the torsion, introduced solely to close gaps on the number line. It appears in (13) and (14) and is set to 1 everywhere in this document, including in Figure 2.

It is left alone because it is the construction's one genuinely undetermined piece, and pretending otherwise would misrepresent the arc. With λ unconstrained, closures exist at every decay exponent and the geometry forbids nothing; with λ ≡ 1, the rigidity claim is exactly the Riemann Hypothesis. Everything interesting lives in the middle, in an admissible class nobody has pinned down — loose enough to contain the mollifiers and the heat flow, tight enough to exclude conspiracies. rh4 §18 states that as the concrete problem the construction leaves open, and it is still open.

The same applies to the two conditions §1 of rh4 states without using: the growth condition Δϑn + Δϑn+1 ≤ Δϑn+2 on the orientation increments, and the monotonicity of the absolute orientations — the gear train turns one way and never slackens. Neither does any work in this document. rh4 §13 and §18 make what they can of them.

Part IV · Onward

13 · Where this goes

The construction, complete, is a language with no theorem in it. What the rest of the arc does with it, in one line each:

  • rh4 — the dictionary. Every notion above pushed to its sharpest counterpart in analytic number theory: the dual helix is the functional equation, the critical line is where a helix and its dual are congruent rather than merely similar, the rotating frame that flattens the helix is Riemann–Siegel Z, and the missing axiom is named — an inner product with provable positivity.
  • rh5 — the clock's structure group (ℤ/12, entirely abelian, which is the diagnosis of why the missing pairing has nowhere to live), the b-helix of §10, and the invisible cone of §11.
  • rh6 — the cone corrected into the horn, and a ninth axiom: strictly monotone winding.
  • rh10, rh11 — Weil's positivity criterion built as an instrument, and the finite-field case where square-root rigidity is a theorem, isolating precisely what the circles do not supply.
  • rh19–rh27a — the machinery: a certified column of positive-definiteness floors for one window, at one support, computed in interval arithmetic.
  • rh28, rh29 — where it stands. Nine certified points, still measure zero; the first measurement of how the floor decays with depth, and the news is worse than a power law.

Read in that order the arc is a single long argument that a good language is not a strategy. This document is the language's first page, written last.
