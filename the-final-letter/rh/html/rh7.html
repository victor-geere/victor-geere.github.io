The primes of the 2π base

The sieve of sines, run inside the base-2π numeral: why a radix cannot move a prime, what the sieve turns out to be sieving instead, and why the p-row of the sieve is literally the factorization of p.

Abstract. rh6 §1 made the numeral positional in base 2π: the station angle is the residue, the winding is the carry. This document runs rh5 §1's sine sieve inside that base. The direct answer is a negative one and it is worth stating first: the primes are unchanged — the first-miss cascade in base 2π is {2}→3→5→7→11→13, computed here twice, once on integers and once natively on rotations. The sieve reads periods, and a period is a count, not an angle; 2π is a unit of angle and never enters the count (§1). The substance is in what the sieve is sieving once it has been moved there. Gears become rotations ξ, with row sin(πxξ), and they fall into three classes (§2): irrational ξ never closes at all — verified to x ≤ 2×10⁵ — so Lebesgue-almost every gear is invisible; rational ξ = a/q closes exactly on qℤ, independent of the numerator; hence the sieve's entire visible world is the torsion subgroup ℚ/ℤ and primality lives in the denominator. The primes of the 2π base are therefore the rotations of prime order — the primitive p-th roots of unity. And then the sieve row turns out to be an old object in disguise (§3–4): |1 − ζpk| = 2 sin(πk/p), so rh5's chord identity ∏ 2 sin(πk/p) = p is Φp(1) = p; the quotients (1−ζk)/(1−ζ) are units; and p = unit · λpp−1 with λp = 1 − ζp — in the 2π base p is not prime but totally ramified, and the genuine prime's modulus 2 sin(π/p) is the row's opening chord. Only p = 2 is its own cyclotomic prime, which is the half-turn axiom. Dually (§5), ℚ/ℤ ≅ ⊕p ℤ(p∞) is Pontryagin dual to the gear train Ẑ of rh5 §5: the primary decomposition is unique factorization, dualized, and Kronecker–Weber places the whole base at ℚab with Galois group Ẑ×. Taken literally as a radix (§6), 2π is transcendental, hence not a Parry number, hence no integer expansion terminates or repeats: 2, 3, 5 are single digits, 7 is the first prime needing a carry, and in base 2π the primes keep their primality and lose their names.

Part I · The negative answer

1 · The answer, and why a radix cannot move a prime

The question is direct, so answer it directly. The primes in the 2π base are the primes: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … The sieve's first-miss cascade was run here twice — once on integers as in rh5 §1, and once natively, enrolling rotations rather than divisors — and both produce

(1) {2} ↦ 3,   {2,3} ↦ 5,   {2,3,5} ↦ 7,   {2,3,5,7} ↦ 11,   {2,3,5,7,11} ↦ 13.

Nothing moved. The reason is worth stating in the construction's own vocabulary, because it is not a coincidence of this particular sieve. The sieve enrols a gear and asks when that gear closes — returns to its starting tooth. Closure is counted in clicks: gear p closes at click n exactly when p | n. A period is therefore a count, an element of ℕ. But 2π is a unit of angle. It measures the circle the gear turns on; it never appears in the tally of how many clicks the gear took. Changing the base rescales the ruler laid along the number line, and primality is not a property of the ruler.

Why no radix can do it

A radix is a choice of additive bookkeeping: it fixes how a number is assembled from place values by addition. Primality is a statement about multiplicative structure — the impossibility of a factorization. The two structures are related only through distributivity, which every base shares. So "the primes in base b" is, for any b whatsoever, the same set of numbers wearing different names. This is why the interesting question is not which numbers are prime in the 2π base, but which objects the sieve is acting on once it has been moved there — and there the answer does change.

Part II · What the sieve actually sieves

2 · What the sieve sieves there: three kinds of rotation

In the base-2π numeral the natural object is not an integer p but a rotation. Let a gear advance by angle α per click and write ξ = α/2π for its rotation number. After x clicks it stands at angle 2πxξ, so its chord distance from closure is 2R|sin(πxξ)|: the sieve row is

(2) sξ(x) = sin(πxξ),    with rh5's row sin(πx/p) the special case ξ = 1/p.

Now the rotations sort themselves into three classes, and the sorting is the whole content of this section.

Irrational ξ: never closes. If ξ is irrational then xξ is never an integer for x ≥ 1, so sξ(x) ≠ 0 always. Such a gear sieves nothing: it deletes no integer, announces no prime, and contributes no row to the product. Since the rationals are Lebesgue-null in the circle, almost every rotation is invisible to the sieve. This is rh5 §6's breach theorem arriving from the other side: there, exact incidence was shown to be reserved to a discrete lattice; here that same lattice is exactly the part of the circle the sieve can read at all.

Rational ξ = a/q: closes on qℤ, and the numerator is invisible. With gcd(a, q) = 1, sin(πxa/q) = 0 ⟺ q | xa ⟺ q | x. Verified here for every reduced a/q with q ≤ 24: all φ(q) rotations of denominator q have identical zero sets. The gear's speed is the numerator and its period is the denominator, and the sieve — which reads only closures — sees the period alone.

The relocation

The sieve's visible world in the 2π base is the torsion subgroup ℚ/ℤ of the circle: the rotations of finite order. On that world the sieve is the sieve of Eratosthenes run on denominators. Hence the answer to the question as an object-level question: the primes of the 2π base are the rotations of prime order — the primitive p-th roots of unity e2πia/p, of which there are φ(p) = p − 1 for each prime p, all sieving identically.

3 · The prime rotations, and the row as a factorization

Having moved the primes onto the circle, one identity does all the remaining work. For any root of unity, |1 − eiθ| = 2|sin(θ/2)|, so with ζp = e2πi/p,

(3) |1 − ζpk| = 2 sin(πk/p) :

the sieve's p-row, sampled at the clicks k = 1, …, p−1, is precisely the list of distances from the point 1 to the other p-th roots of unity — the chords of Figure 2. Verified to 10−13 for p ≤ 13. And rh5's chord identity, which there looked like a pleasant fact about polygons, is now recognizable:

(4) p−1∏k=1 2 sin(πk/p)  =  p−1∏k=1 (1 − ζpk)  =  Φp(1)  =  p,

where Φp(x) = 1 + x + ⋯ + xp−1 is the p-th cyclotomic polynomial. Both products computed to 10−9 for p = 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. The p-row of the sine sieve is the factorization of p in the 2π base, written out chord by chord — not an analogy for it, the thing itself. What rh5 §1 could only say as "place value is the circle's own trigonometry" is here the exact statement that the row multiplies to the integer it belongs to.

4 · Total ramification, and the seed gear as fixed point

Equation (4) contains more than a product. Each factor is the shortest chord times a ratio,

(5) 1 − ζk1 − ζ = 1 + ζ + ⋯ + ζk−1,

and every one of these ratios is a unit: its norm is ∏j(1 − ζjk)/∏j(1 − ζj) = p/p = 1, verified numerically to 10−8 for every k and every p ≤ 13. Writing λp = 1 − ζp, equation (4) collapses to

(6) p = (unit) · λpp−1,    |λp| = 2 sin(π/p).
The primes are not where they were

In the 2π base p is not a prime element at all: it is a (p−1)-st power. The genuine prime is λp = 1 − ζp, and p is totally ramified — the ramification index equals the full degree p−1 of the extension. This is classical (Washington, Cyclotomic Fields, Lemma 1.4); what the geometry contributes is the reading: the modulus of the true prime is the opening chord of the sieve's row, 2 sin(π/p), the first sample the sieve ever takes of gear p.

The seed gear is the fixed point. At p = 2 the exponent p−1 is 1 and the chord is the diameter: λ2 = 1 − eiπ = 1 − (−1) = 2, exactly. So 2 is the one prime that is already its own cyclotomic prime, unramified and unmoved by the change of base — and 1 − eiπ is nothing but the phase-opposite axiom of rh4, the half-turn, written as an algebraic element. The axiom that rh5 §1 found seeding the sieve is the same object that here refuses to ramify. Every other prime disperses into p−1 conjugate chords; the half-turn stands alone.

One further identity closes the picture, and it is the sieve's bookkeeping made exact. The closure poset — which gears close at which clicks — is the divisor lattice, and cyclotomic polynomials factor along it:

(7) xn − 1 =  ∏d | n Φd(x),

verified at n = 6, 12, 30, 36. Reading it in the geometry: the rotations that close by click n are partitioned by their exact order d, one cyclotomic factor per divisor — rh5 §1's statement that a gear's closures are its multiples, promoted from a set identity to a polynomial one.

Part III · The dual, the radix, the balance

5 · The dual picture: ℚ/ℤ against Ẑ

rh5 §5 identified the gear train's state space as the profinite integers Ẑ = lim← ℤ/m. The sieve's visible world here is the torsion subgroup ℚ/ℤ. These are not two unrelated finds: ℚ/ℤ is the Pontryagin dual of Ẑ. The train's states and the rotations that read them are dual descriptions of one object — which is why the sieve, moved from one to the other, returned the same primes.

The duality carries the arithmetic across intact. Every torsion rotation splits uniquely into prime-power parts,

(8) ℚ/ℤ ≅  ⊕p ℤ(p∞),    e.g. 116/231 = 1/3 + 5/7 + 5/11 (mod 1),

the primary decomposition into Prüfer groups — verified here for every k/n with n ∈ {12, 30, 36, 60, 231}. This is unique factorization, dualized: where the train factors a count into prime powers, the circle splits a rotation into prime-order components, and the Chinese remainder theorem is the same theorem read in either direction. The sieve's inability to see numerators is the group-theoretic statement that ℤ(p∞)'s subgroups are totally ordered — only the order matters, never which generator.

Globally, the base has a name. Adjoining all the prime rotations to ℚ generates ℚ(ζ∞), and by Kronecker–Weber that is exactly ℚab, the maximal abelian extension — with Gal(ℚab/ℚ) ≅ Ẑ×, the units of the gear train. So the 2π base is not an eccentric choice of ruler; it is the abelian closure of the rationals, and its symmetry group is the invertible part of the object rh5 §5 already named. This is also the ceiling: rh5's diagnosis was that every symmetry the construction presents is abelian, and Kronecker–Weber says the 2π base reaches precisely as far as abelian goes and not one step further. The missing representation is still missing, and now one knows exactly which wall it is behind.

6 · 2π taken literally as a radix

The remaining reading is the most literal: use 2π as an actual radix, expanding numbers as ∑ dk(2π)k. This is the theory of β-expansions (Rényi 1957, Parry 1960): the greedy algorithm gives every positive real a representation with digits in {0, …, ⌈β⌉−1}, here {0, …, 6}. Two facts settle its character immediately. 2π is transcendental (Lindemann 1882), hence not an algebraic integer, hence not a Perron number; and every Parry number is Perron. So 2π is not a Parry number: the β-shift is not sofic, and no integer's expansion terminates or falls into a cycle.

The consequence is the exact inverse of what a radix is normally for. A base exists to give numbers short, checkable names — in base ten primality is not visible in the digits either, but at least the digits stop. Here they do not. In base 2π the primes keep their primality and lose their names. That is not a defect of the sieve; it is the reason the sieve had to be moved onto rotations in §2 rather than run on digit strings. The digit expansion is the additive face of the base and it is hostile to arithmetic; the torsion rotations are the multiplicative face and they carry the arithmetic perfectly.

7 · What this settles

The question was what the sine sieve yields in the base-2π numeral, and the answer has three layers that should not be collapsed into each other.

At the level of numbers: nothing changes. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, …, cascade for cascade. A radix is additive bookkeeping and primality is multiplicative; no choice of base can touch it, and it is worth having the reason stated cleanly rather than discovered by accident (§1).

At the level of objects: everything changes. The sieve stops acting on integers and starts acting on rotations; almost all of them are invisible to it; the ones it can read are the torsion ℚ/ℤ; and the primes among those are the prime-order roots of unity (§2). This is the honest content of "primes in the 2π base".

At the level of arithmetic: the geometry turns out to have been carrying cyclotomic theory all along. The chord identity of rh5 §1 is Φp(1) = p; the row is the factorization; p is totally ramified with the true prime's modulus equal to the row's opening chord; the seed gear p = 2 is the unique fixed point, and it is the half-turn axiom (§3–4). Dually the gear train Ẑ and the rotation circle ℚ/ℤ are Pontryagin partners, the primary decomposition is unique factorization, and Kronecker–Weber names the base ℚab with Galois group Ẑ× (§5).

That last point is where this document connects back to the arc. rh5 §5 diagnosed the missing Axiom 8 as a missing representation: every symmetry on the table is abelian, and abelian symmetry has no room for a nontrivial positivity. The 2π base does not repair that, and §5 explains why it cannot — Kronecker–Weber says this base is the abelian world, exactly and completely. Moving the sieve onto the circle buys a genuinely better description of what the primes are; it buys no new symmetry, because there is none left of that kind to buy. The construction's ceiling and the 2π base's ceiling are the same ceiling.
