The circles, pressed further

Six questions put to the nested-circle geometry — a sieve of sines, the b-helix dual, the invisible cone, ZFC restated on the circles, the structure group named, and the torsions that break the axioms.

docs/rh5 · answers the open problems of docs/rh4 · figures computed, not sketched · 2026-07-31

Abstract. The companion document rh4 ended with a precise ledger of debts: the one missing axiom is an inner product with provable positivity (rh4 §15), the admissible retuning class must be pinned down, and the axioms need quantitative teeth (rh4 §18). This document works six further questions against that ledger. A proposed sieve of sines is repaired and identified: run correctly it is Eratosthenes, and weighted it is Euler's product (§1). The b-helix is shown to be a congruent, non-annihilating dual of the a-helix — a rigid rotation by π/6 sharing every closure — and the definite-sign pairing it furnishes is computed, then honestly classified as the free kind that forces nothing (§2). The discrete helix is smoothed onto its invisible cone: the continuum limb evaluates to Γ-factors with no zeros at all, so every closure is lattice interference; the exact bridge is the Fermi–Dirac integral for η, verified to 10−14 and vanishing at the first zero (§3). The ZFC axioms are restated circle by circle, and the hypothesis' logical altitude is computed: RH is Π⁰₁ — a gear-congestion bound (Robin), checked here to 10⁶ — hence absolute under forcing and true if undecidable (§4). The construction's structure group is assembled — ℤ/12 clock, profinite gear train Ẑ, U(1) torsion, GL(1) characters — and found entirely abelian, which is the Lie-theoretic diagnosis of why the missing pairing has nowhere to live in the present axioms (§5). Finally the torsion states that breach the axioms are classified: exact incidence survives only on a whole-tone lattice of radii, refinement is self-breaching (which is why gap-filling must be approximation in mean — Beurling–Nyman), and the train's own breach resonances are exactly the gauge closures at t = 2πk/log 2 (§6). None of this proves the hypothesis; each answer sharpens where the proof cannot come from.

Part I · The sieve and the dual

1 · The sieve of sines, repaired and run

The proposal: primes should appear as the integers missed by an expanding family of sines — begin with sin(πx/2) + sin(πx/4) + …, whose roots miss 3; adjoin the multiples of 1/3, and the roots miss 5; adjoin 1/5, and they miss 7. The instinct is exactly right, and two repairs make it exact.

Two corrections

Sum → product. Roots of a sum of sines are not the union of the separate root sets: superposition interferes. Computed: sin(πx/2) + sin(πx/4) equals 1 at x = 2 — the wanted root is destroyed — and vanishes at x = 8/3 — a spurious root appears (both to 10−15). The sieve needs the union of zero sets, which is the product ∏p sin(πx/p), or simply the family kept as separate rows (Figure 1).

The doublings are redundant — as sets. Every root of sin(πx/4) is already a root of sin(πx/2): multiples of 4 are multiples of 2. Only a new prime frequency contributes new integer roots — this redundancy is unique factorization speaking (rh4 §7). The doublings 1/4, 1/8, … do earn their keep, but as weights, not sets: the explicit formula counts the 2-gear's deeper closings 4, 8, 16, … with von Mangoldt weight Λ(2k) = log 2 (rh4 §8). What is redundant for sieving is exactly what carries the prime-power bookkeeping.

Run correctly, the engine is a loop. Keep a set P of enrolled frequencies; call x covered if some enrolled sin(πx/p) vanishes there; the first uncovered integer ≥ 2 is announced, and enrolled. Computed: {2} misses 3; {2, 3} misses 5; {2, 3, 5} misses 7; {2, 3, 5, 7} misses 11. This is the sieve of Eratosthenes verbatim — each prime exists first as a failure of coverage, and only afterwards as a gear. (Eratosthenes' classical optimization is here too: below p², missing already means prime, because a composite has a factor at most its square root.)

The geometry underneath is the gear train. On a circle with p teeth, the chord from the position at continuous click x back to the starting tooth has length 2R |sin(πx/p)|: the sine is the gear's distance from closure, made continuous. A root at integer n is the statement "gear p closes at click n", i.e. p | n. The sieve's rows are the closure combs of the prime gears, and the polygon axiom is quantified by a classical identity the construction should own: the product of all chords from one vertex of the regular n-gon is

(1) n−1∏k=1 2 sin(πk/n) = n,

verified here for n ≤ 50 to 10−9: each circle's polygon carries the integer it denotes as chord data. Place value is not painted on the circle; it is the circle's own trigonometry.

One gear is never announced: the loop cannot start empty. The construction seeds it by axiom — the phase-opposite rule a + A = 0 is the 2-row, since the half-turn alternation (−1)n−1 is exactly the sign of the fundamental sine at half-integer phase. The geometry decrees precisely one prime; every other prime is discovered as a miss. Figure 1 marks the seed row "axiom 5".

Now attach the Dirichlet weight n−s and the sieve becomes a ledger of the train's presentations. Deleting the multiples of p multiplies the train by (1 − p−s). Flipping them with weight p — the η-style move — multiplies by (1 − p1−s): for p = 2 this is the alternating helix itself, and for every p it is the gauge family of rh4 §3 (verified numerically for p = 2, 3 at s = 2.3 to 10−8). Run the deletions over all primes and the sieve completes:

(2) ζ(s)  ∏p (1 − p−s) = 1 :

every gear sieved out, only the empty numeral survives. Euler's product is Eratosthenes run at complex weight. Computed: ζ(2) ∏p ≤ 1000(1 − p−2) = 1.000127. Two classical consequences fall out of the completed sieve and belong to the geometry. The sieve never idles — Euler (1737): if the primes were finite the product would be finite while the harmonic train diverges; so the gear supply is infinite. And the sieve has a density — Mertens (1874): ∏p ≤ x(1 − 1/p)−1 = eγ log x (1 + o(1)); computed at x = 10⁴ the ratio is 1.0012. Hold on to that constant eγ: it returns in §4 as the ceiling of the gear-congestion form of RH, and the coincidence is a theorem (Gronwall), not an accident — both numbers measure the multiplicative mass of the full train.

What the sieve does not supply is as important. It yields no formula for the next prime — only a process whose next announcement is unknowable until the previous gears have been consulted. The compensating global law is the explicit formula (rh4 §8): the closures of the train, taken all together, resum exactly what the announcements refuse to say locally. The sieve is the local face, the zeros the global face, of the same arithmetic; RH is a statement about the fairness of the global face.

2 · The b-helix: a dual that does not annihilate

The clock has twelve digits a … l at spacing π/6, and the half-turn is six steps: the antipode of digit k is k + 6. That gives six axes {a, A}, {b, B}, …, with the identifications the construction already made honestly: the letter at a's antipode is g, so g ≡ A; likewise h ≡ B. The g-string was retired as the exact negative of the a-string; the b-string is the first genuinely new choice — adjacent to a, not opposite it.

Under cumulative half-turns the string bbbb… presents, level by level, as bBbB… — exactly as aaaa… presents as aAaA… — and its value is a rigid rotation of the value of the a-helix:

(3) val(bbbb…) = ∞∑n=1 (−1)n−1 λn rn eiρn · eiπ/6 = eiπ/6 η(s).

Congruent — the same helix turned 30° — and not annihilating: the partner that cancels a is A alone. All twelve digit strings give eikπ/6 η(s): one orbit of congruent copies. The immediate consequence is that the zero set is digit-independent. A closure is not an accident of the letter a; it is the simultaneous collapse of the entire clock frame — all twelve helices returning to the origin at once. Figure 2 draws the a- and b-paths at the first zero: two congruent spirals, 30° apart, closing together.

Is it the pairing with definite sign?

The question was whether this non-annihilating dual should be the missing axiom — a pairing of helices with definite sign. Compute it. Taking the natural sesquilinear pairing of values,

(4) ⟨a, b⟩ = val(aaaa…) · val(bbbb…) = e−iπ/6 |η(s)|²,    Re ⟨a, b⟩ = cos(π/6) |η(s)|² ≥ 0,

verified numerically to 10−12, with equality exactly at closures. So yes: the b-helix furnishes a pairing of definite sign, vanishing precisely on the zero set. And now the litmus test of rh4 §15 must be applied, because it is merciless: a pairing that the Davenport–Heilbronn function also possesses cannot be the axiom that forbids Davenport–Heilbronn's off-line zeros. The b-pairing fails the test. Its positivity is the free positivity of a norm-square — cos(π/6)|F(s)|² ≥ 0 holds for every Dirichlet series F whatsoever, Euler product or not, RH or not. It is evaluated one point at a time and constrains nothing about where the vanishing happens.

The diagnosis, stated once and used again in §5: definite sign is cheap pointwise and expensive spectrally. Weil's pairing (rh4 §15, eq. 9) is a sum over all closures against prime-gear data; its positivity is exactly RH. The b-pairing is a single |η|² in disguise; its positivity is exactly nothing. Between the two lies the entire hypothesis. There is one respect in which the b-copy is more than decoration: an inner product, when it exists, is recoverable from norms of rotated combinations (polarization), and the clock supplies every rotated copy one could ask for. The clock is polarization-ready — what is missing is the space on which to polarize, and §5 says why the present axioms cannot supply it.

The de-twisted dual

One more object hides in the b-axis, and it teaches the opposite lesson. Choose the digit alternately: b on odd circles, B on even. The digit alternation now cancels the frame's half-turn — (−1)n−1 from the frame, (−1)n−1 from the string, product one — and the value degenerates to constant phase:

(5) val(bBbB… as chosen digits) = eiπ/6 ∞∑n=1 λn rn eiρn  =  the un-alternated ζ train,

which diverges everywhere in the strip. Computed at s = ½ + iγ₁: the partial sums grow like √N/|1−s| — predicted 7.0704 at N = 10⁴, measured 7.0705, and doubling as N quadruples. Choosing digits to fight the frame switches analytic continuation off: the strip becomes invisible, exactly the lesson of rh4 §2 run in reverse. The b-axis thus carries both duals at once: the congruent dual (ride the frame; keep every closure, keep the strip) and the de-twisted dual (fight the frame; lose both). Only the first deserves the name.

Part II · Continuum and foundations

3 · The invisible cone and the integral forms

Promote the level index to a continuous variable: radius r(x) = x−σ, phase ϑ(x) = (x−1)π − t log x, for x ∈ [1, ∞). This is the a-line drawn on the invisible cone: the surface that interpolates the nested circles, with the continuous monotone torsion of the axioms in place of discrete jumps. Figure 3 (left) draws it in plan view. With torsion off (t = 0) it is the clean half-turn spiral threading every a/A pole; at t = γ₁ the torsion shears it, and one feature appears that the discrete helix can only hint at: the phase velocity ϑ′(x) = π − t/x is negative until

(6) x* = t/π   (= 4.4992… at t = γ₁):

below x* the torsion overpowers the half-turns and the cone path winds backwards; at x* it reverses. This is the construction's own conductor scale — the raw, pre-symmetrization cousin of the effective gear count √(t/2π) of rh4 §6.

What does the continuum evaluate to? Replace the sum over circles by the integral over the cone. The bare cone integral is a classical exercise in rotating the contour onto the imaginary axis (the Fresnel–Γ integral, valid for 0 < σ < 1):

(7) ∞∫0 eiπx x−s dx = eiπ(1−s)/2 Γ(1−s) πs−1,

whose rotated real form is verified here against Stirling to 4×10−17, with the pretty special case that the modulus at s = ½ is exactly 1. The right-hand side is Γ-factors and exponentials — and Γ has no zeros. The smeared train never closes. Not at the gauge points, not at γ₁, nowhere: the continuum limb of the construction is zero-free, and even the trivial zeros enter only through the reflection's sine. Every closure the numeral has — trivial and nontrivial alike — is an interference effect of the integer lattice, invisible to the cone.

The exact ledger between cone and circles is the eta integral the construction should adopt as its own. Resumming the circles' profiles, ∑ (−1)n−1 e−nt = 1/(et + 1), gives the Fermi–Dirac representation, valid on all of σ > 0:

(8) η(s) Γ(s) = ∞∫0 ts−1et + 1 dt.

Computed: exact at s = 2 (π²/12, to 2×10−15); equal to η·Γ at ½ + 2i to 2×10−14; and at s = ½ + iγ₁ the integral is 3×10−15 — at a closure, the cone's oscillating cross-section has exactly zero signed area (Figure 3, right). The alternation sits inside the kernel as the +1 in the denominator: half-turns are a fermionic filling rule, the Pauli sign of the construction; the plain ζ train has the Bose kernel 1/(et − 1) and pays for it with the pole at s = 1. And the "invisible cone" is not a metaphor Riemann lacked: his 1859 continuation is the loop integral ζ(s) = Γ(1−s)/2πi ∮ (−z)s−1/(ez−1) dz, the contour wrapped around the cone's axis — the Hankel loop is the cone made rigorous.

The implication cuts against a natural hope. One might try to prove rigidity by smoothing: replace the train by the cone, control the difference. But the cone carries no zeros at all — the zeros are the difference. The sum-minus-integral discrepancy is governed by Poisson summation, whose dual lattice produces both the functional equation (Jacobi's θ(1/x) = √x θ(x), the route Riemann took) and the zeros themselves. Discreteness is not a defect the cone smooths away; it is the sole source of everything the hypothesis is about — which is why the hard theorems (subconvexity, mollification, Λ ≥ 0) all live in the oscillating lattice term, and why the coin-flip boundary σ = ½ of rh4 §9 is a statement about lattice signs, not about any smooth limb.

4 · ZFC on the circles, and the altitude of RH

The nesting order is a membership relation. Read "circle m lies strictly inside circle n" as m ∈ n, and the nest realizes the von Neumann integers: circle n is the set {0, 1, …, n−1} of the circles it contains, the innermost point is ∅, and the whole tower is ω. Under that reading each ZFC axiom has a circle-side face:

ZFCOn the circles
Extensionalitya gear is its interior: two circles enclosing the same sub-train are the same circle — the radius is retuned from the contents (axiom 7's λ)

Choicea digit string is a choice function, one letter per circle; the helix bundle of §2 is the space of such choices, and countable choice is all the numeral ever uses

The drawable ends at Power set. The collection of all infinite digit strings is the Cantor space 12ω — the boundary of the digit tree, not a circle in any nest. That is the first genuinely second-order object the construction touches, and it is where the analysis of rh4 lives (mollifier classes, admissible retunings, the universality of rh4 §17, which says the helix tail is dense in that boundary's function-space shadow). Essentially all of classical analytic number theory formalizes in second-order arithmetic. The hypothesis itself sits far lower — and this is the section's real content.

Theorem (the altitude of RH)

RH is a Π⁰₁ statement of arithmetic: a single universal quantifier over a decidable matrix. Two certificates. Analytic: RH ⟺ |ψ(x) − x| ≤ √x log²x / 8π for all x ≥ 73.2 (Schoenfeld's effective von Koch) — each instance a finite computation over the gears. Purely gear-side: RH ⟺ σ(n) < eγ n log log n for every n > 5040 (Robin), with Lagarias's unconditional dressing σ(n) ≤ Hn + eHn log Hn for all n ≥ 1.

In gear language Robin's criterion is a congestion bound. The divisors of n are exactly the gears that close at click n, so σ(n) = ∑d|n d is the total circumference of gears closing together, and RH says the simultaneous-closure traffic never exceeds the Mertens density ceiling eγ n log log n — the same eγ as §1's sieve density, and Gronwall's theorem (lim sup σ(n)/(n log log n) = eγ) is the bridge: the busiest clicks approach the ceiling forever without, if RH holds, ever touching it. (the champions are the colossally abundant clicks, as they must be), with Lagarias's inequality verified for all n ≤ 10⁴. None of this is evidence in the asymptotic sense rh4 §18 warned about; it is the shape of the statement that matters, and the shape has two consequences worth owning precisely.

If RH is false, it is provably false — one overloaded click, checked by finite computation, refutes it. Contrapositively: if RH is undecidable in PA, or in ZFC, it is true. There is no permanent limbo in which the hypothesis floats unresolved as a matter of fact; Π⁰₁ statements do not enjoy the independence CH enjoys. And forcing cannot touch it: arithmetic statements are absolute between a universe of sets and all its forcing extensions (and, via Shoenfield, well beyond). No Cohen-style expansion of the set-theoretic sky changes whether the gear train conspires. The circles — which under the nesting reading are ω — carry the entire truth of RH by themselves; the universe of sets above them can contribute proof-power, never truth. That is the honest sense of "expanding the axioms to set theory": ordinals beyond ω exist in the geometry as nests of nests (ω·2, ω², … — numeral systems indexing numeral systems), and they may be needed to prove the bound; they cannot be needed to make it.

Part III · Symmetry and breach

5 · The structure group: clock, gears, torsion

What group does the construction actually act by? Assemble it layer by layer, because the answer — and the disappointment it encodes — is the sharpest thing this document has to say about the missing axiom.

The clock is ℤ/12. Digits form the cyclic group of order twelve; the half-turn is the element 6; the six axes of §2 are the cosets of {0, 6}. Rotating the digit choice by one step multiplies val by eiπ/6: the twelve helices are one orbit, and the value map is a character of the clock. The alternation itself is the choice of a character odd on the half-turn — that is all the phase-opposite axiom is, representation-theoretically.

The gear train is Ẑ. The full state of the train at click n is the tuple (n mod m)m — one residue per gear — and the space of all coherent states is the inverse limit Ẑ = lim← ℤ/m ≅ ∏p ℤp: the gear train is a physical model of the profinite integers (CRT verified here as the 60 = 4·3·5 bijection). Arithmetic progressions are the clopen sets of this topology; Fürstenberg's topological proof of the infinitude of primes (1955) is an open-cover argument about exactly this space, and §1's sieve is its constructive shadow.

The torsion is U(1), the radii are ℝ₊. Level x acts by (x−σ, e−it log x) ∈ ℝ₊ × U(1) = ℂ×. The whole numeral is the character χs(n) = n−s of the multiplicative monoid (ℕ, ×), which unique factorization makes free on the primes (rh4 §7) — and a character of a free monoid is determined by arbitrary values on the generators. Euler's product is character theory: ζ(s) = ∏ (1 − χs(p))−1 is the statement that the character factors through the generators.

The Lie algebra is 𝔤𝔩(1). Lie(ℂ×) = ℂ, spanned by the dilation generator D (radial) and the rotation generator Θ (torsion); the parameter s = σ + it is precisely the slope σD + tΘ of a one-parameter subgroup, composed with log. The torsion axiom "continuous and monotone" says: use the same one-parameter subgroup at every level. Berry–Keating's H = ½(xp + px) is this D made self-adjoint on the half-line (rh4 §13). The half-turn rule is a connection on the level line with holonomy π per unit — on a one-dimensional base curvature has nowhere to live, so the connection is its holonomy, the helix is parallel transport, and the invariants of such a connection are exactly its holonomy characters. This is why everything the construction produces keeps being a character: η's alternation, the gauge family, the twelve congruent helices. At this altitude nothing else exists.

The reflection is a Weyl group. The dual-helix involution s ↦ 1−s is the Weyl group ℤ/2 of a rank-one spectral problem, and not metaphorically: ζ sits in the constant term of the SL(2, ℤ) Eisenstein series, ys + (ξ(2s−1)/ξ(2s)) y1−s, where the functional equation is the Weyl reflection and the critical line is the unitary axis. The congruence-only-on-the-line computation of rh4 §5 is the rank-one tempered condition in circle clothes.

The diagnosis

Every symmetry now on the table — ℤ/12, Ẑ, U(1), ℝ₊, the Weyl ℤ/2 — is abelian. Abelian groups have only one-dimensional irreducible representations, and on a one-dimensional space an invariant sesquilinear pairing is a scalar: there is no cone of positive pairings to choose from, no signature, no index — nothing for an Axiom 8 to grip. Weil's positivity was purchased on H¹ of a curve: a 2g-dimensional space with a genuine intersection form on a surface (rh4 §14). The pairing had room. The missing axiom is therefore not a missing formula but a missing representation: a space on which these abelian symmetries act with multiplicity, where "positive" can mean something. That is a precise description of where every serious program already digs — Hilbert–Pólya's operator space, Connes' adele-class space (built exactly on Ẑ × ℝ₊ above), de Branges' spaces, the function-field H¹. In Lie terms: 𝔤𝔩(1) must be represented, not enlarged.

6 · Breaking torsions: the breach census

Use the structure to build a number line with scaling factor ½: position ξ on the line is the circle of radius 2−ξ, and the helix descends with the standard torsion, a half-turn per unit of ξ, meeting the circle for ξ at angle πξ. The incidence axiom — helices meet circles at polygon vertices, the digits — sanctions only angles in (π/6)ℤ. So the permissible stations are

(9) ξ ∈ ⅙ℤ  ⟺  radii 2−k/6 :

a whole-tone lattice — successive permissible radii are 200 cents apart (computed exactly), six stations per octave of halving, so each octave carries one of the two whole-tone scales and the clock alternates scales octave by octave. The twelve digits are exhausted after two halvings. The geometry, asked to carry a ruler, answers with equal temperament.

Everything off that lattice breaches. The first casualty is the line's own children: ξ = ¼ — the midpoint of the first halving — meets its circle at π/4, which is π/12 from digit b and π/12 from digit c (Figure 4, left): the helix passes through the circle between two permissible points, the exact violation the question names. And no fixed torsion repairs it: serving every dyadic ξ = j/2m would require T·j/2m ∈ (π/6)ℤ for all m, forcing T = 0. Refinement is self-breaching: the halving line cannot host its own midpoints. Exact incidence is reserved to a discrete lattice forever; the continuum can only be approached in mean. That is not a defect discovered here — it is the reason the gap-closing axiom exists, and it is precisely the form RH takes in Beurling–Nyman (rh4 §16): approximation of the constant function in L², never pointwise incidence. The breach theorem is the geometric face of why that criterion is about closures of subspaces and not about hitting points.

What if the torsion is retuned? A rational ratio T/(π/6) lands periodically and breaches periodically. An irrational ratio never exactly lands again: by Weyl equidistribution (1916) the crossings fill the circle uniformly and the permissible set has density zero — the axioms break almost surely. But the near-misses are as ordered as non-incidence permits: by the three-distance theorem (Steinhaus's problem; Sós and Świerczkowski, 1958–59), at any finite stage the crossing angles cut the circle into gaps of at most three distinct lengths, the largest the sum of the other two. Computed at golden torsion, forty crossings: gaps 0.01316, 0.02129, 0.03444 of the circle, and 0.01316 + 0.02129 = 0.03444 to 10−9. Breach structure is rigid even in failure.

Now let the train diagnose itself. Scaling the whole nest by 2 shifts every gear by log 2 in clock time, so at height t the halving beats against the half-turn with phase t log 2, and exact resonance — the torsion state in which the doubled train re-enters the digit frame with no offset — occurs at t log 2 ∈ 2πℤ. These are precisely the gauge closures s = 1 + 2πik/log 2 of rh4 §3: the identity |1 − 21−s| = 2|sin(t log 2/2)| on σ = 1 is verified to 10−12, η closes there to 10−15, and an independent Euler–Maclaurin evaluation gives |ζ(1 + it₁)| = 1.351055 — nothing arithmetic vanishes. The axiom-breaking torsion states the question asks for are real, computable, and already catalogued: they break the presentation, not the arithmetic, which is rh4's gauge lesson sharpened into a resonance condition.

Could two gears resonate at once? Never exactly: t log 2 ∈ 2πℤ and t log 3 ∈ 2πℤ together would force log 2/log 3 rational, i.e. 2a = 3b — unique factorization forbids it (the ℚ-independence of {log p}, rh4 §7). All that remains possible is near-resonance of many gears at once, and counting near-breaches of quality δ at height t is the equidistribution ledger of {t log n mod 2π} — whose generating sums ∑ n−it are the ζ partial sums themselves. The census of near-breaches is the growth of |ζ(½ + it)|: convexity bounds it crudely, a century of subconvexity bounds it better, Lindelöf says the census is subpolynomial, and the statement that the incidence failures never conspire beyond the square-root law is RH in the coin-flip form of rh4 §9. The breach census does not prove anything; it re-locates the hypothesis exactly, as the extreme statement of incidence fairness. One last discrete guarantee stands behind all of it: a torsion that folded the helix through a circle back into an inner gear would create a cycle in the nesting order, and Foundation (§4's table) forbids it — the set-theoretic axioms police the continuum precisely at the point where the breach analysis shows the continuum pressing hardest.

Part IV · The balance

7 · What the six answers add up to

Each question was answered on its own terms; together they triangulate. The sieve (§1) shows the primes entering the geometry only as process — announcements of failure, never a formula — with Euler's product as the completed sieve; so the arithmetic side of any future axiom must be stated over the gear train as a whole, not gear by gear. The b-helix (§2) shows that definite-sign pairings are free at a point and meaningful only spectrally; candidate Axiom 8s that check level by level are disqualified in advance. The cone (§3) shows the continuum limb is zero-free: whatever forbids off-line closures must live in the lattice's interference term, because there is nowhere else for it to live. The foundations (§4) show the hypothesis is Π⁰₁ — decided by the gear train itself, immune to forcing, true if undecidable — so the work is arithmetic, however high the machinery reaches. The structure group (§5) shows every present symmetry is abelian, and abelian symmetry cannot carry a nontrivial positivity; the missing object is a representation with room. The breach census (§6) shows exact incidence is measure-zero and its failures are maximally structured, with the train's own resonances exposed as gauge events; the quantitative face of incidence fairness is Lindelöf-to-RH.

Put together: the missing axiom must be spectral (§2), lattice-borne (§3), arithmetically grounded (§4), represented on a space with multiplicity (§5), and stated in mean, not pointwise (§6) — and §1 reminds us it must see all the gears at once. That is not a proof strategy; it is a filter, and it is notable that the programs still standing — Weil positivity, Hilbert–Pólya/Connes, de Branges, the function-field transplant — are exactly the ones that pass it.
