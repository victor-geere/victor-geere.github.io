Two ladders

Ordered: two incommensurable meshes — the question rh19 §6 ranked first. rh8's incommensurability says two meshes with irrational ratio share no null but zero, so a pair should see every zero, and (L) at two meshes might carry the whole statement. Answered: no, and the reason is a theorem with a constant. For every mesh at once, |BΔ(γ)|² ≤ c₀/γ² with c₀ = 2.100246 — a bound with no Δ in it, and attained. A pair fixes deletion, the failure mode that cost rh19 a factor 35 at Δ*: the low-zero weight floor rises 2,857× at a golden pair. Nothing fixes starvation: above γ = 2384 no mesh whatsoever reaches even that floor. Along the way the obvious guess — that a good pair needs a badly approximable ratio — turned out to be false, and what replaces it is smaller and sharper: four specific zeros.

1 · The order

rh19 §6, listing what it owed, in order of how tractable it looked: "(i) Two incommensurable meshes. §4 shows each mesh is blind on its own notch ladder r ∈ (2π/Δ)ℤ, and Δ* shows what total blindness costs. rh8's incommensurability says two meshes with irrational ratio share no null but zero — so (L) at two such meshes may already carry the whole statement. The obstacle is quantitative: detection needs the surviving weight to be large enough, not merely nonzero, and §4's dips show the weights vary by orders. Testing whether a pair of meshes can jointly bound every low-zero weight from below is the cheapest next question, and a negative answer would be as informative as a positive one."

The answer is negative, and it is more informative than expected, because the obstacle turns out not to be the one named. rh19 guessed the problem was quantitative — that the surviving weights might be too small. They are not: a pair lifts the floor by three orders. The problem is structural, and it was visible all along in the shape of B: the hat's transform decays, and no arrangement of lattices can slow it down. §5 states that as a theorem with a certified constant. §§2–4 measure exactly what a pair is worth on the way there, and find that the received wisdom about which pairs are good is wrong.

2 · Incommensurability, for meshes that are all rational

rh8's statement is about irrational ratios. No computer holds one. Every mesh in this arc has been an exact decimal, so every ratio θ = Δ₂/Δ₁ is rational, and two rational meshes always share notches: writing θ = p/q in lowest terms, the ladders N(Δ₁) = (2π/Δ₁)ℤ and N(Δ₂) coincide first at

(1) rjoint  =  2πq/Δ₁,

and nowhere below it. So incommensurability is not a property one can compute with; what one can compute with is whether rjoint lies above the horizon — above the R₀ at which the archimedean integral is cut, and above the heights of the zeros that carry weight. That condition is cheap. At θ = 1/2 the first joint notch is at 52.4 and at θ = 2/3 it is 78.5, both inside R₀ = 600: those pairs are not incommensurable enough at any horizon, and §3 shows they buy nothing. A second mesh specified to four decimals — Δ₂ = 0.1483 against Δ₁ = 0.24, θ = 1483/2400 — puts it at 6.28×104, and each further decimal multiplies that by ten. Rationality is not the obstacle; small denominators are.

This is the first thing the question needed and did not have: a version of "incommensurable" that a certificate can check. The rest of the document uses it.

3 · What a pair buys, and where it buys nothing

The quantity to watch is the weight the kernel gives each zero. rh14 wrote EΔ(v) = Σγ|BΔ(γ)|²cos(γv) on RH, so a zero at height γ enters the mesh's arithmetic with weight |BΔ(γ)|², and at a notch that weight is zero — the mesh cannot hear it. For a pair, the zero is heard if either mesh hears it, so the object is

(2) F(Δ₁, Δ₂)  =  minj ≤ 40 max( |BΔ₁(γj)|², |BΔ₂(γj)|² ).

At Δ = 0.24 alone, F = 1.294×10−10; at Δ* = 0.444521 — rh19's catastrophe, where the first notch lands on the first zero — it is 4.563×10−67, which is zero to every precision that will ever matter. Adding a second mesh at the golden ratio raises it to 3.696×10−7, a factor 2,857; the best ratio in [0.3, 1] is θ* = 0.581214, giving 9.230×10−7, a factor 7,134. So rh19's worry was misplaced: the surviving weight is not marginal, it is three orders up.

The guess that fails. The natural expectation, and the one rh8's incommensurability suggests, is that a good pair needs a badly approximable θ — the golden ratio being the champion. That is false, and the scan says so flatly: θ = 3/5 is as simple a rational as exists, its Diophantine quality q‖qθ‖ is exactly zero, and it gains 2,857× — the same as 1/φ. Meanwhile θ = 1/2 gains 1.0× and θ = 3/4 gains 1.0×: nothing at all. Diophantine quality is not the criterion.

What the criterion is. The floor at a single mesh is set by one zero. At Δ = 0.24 it is zero #32 at γ = 105.447, whose weight is 1.294×10−10 because 2π/Δ = 26.180 and 4.0278 of those notch spacings reaches it. A second mesh rescues that zero unless it too has a notch there — and the same holds for the next few starved zeros. So the failures are indexed by a finite list. Scanning 6000 ratios: 1,020 of them (17.0%) gain less than 10×, and at every single one the responsible zero is one of exactly four — 52.970, 79.337, 103.726, 105.447 — each sitting within 0.0425 of a notch of both meshes, in units of the notch spacing. The set of bad pairs is arithmetic and finite, not Diophantine; it is a property of where four particular zeros happen to lie.

4 · The other side of the same ratio

rh18's identity holds at arbitrary real nodes with a single hat width, so a pair supplies more than two separate statements: the union Δ₁ℤ ∪ Δ₂ℤ is itself a legitimate node set, and 𝒲(g⋆ǧ) on hat-splines over that set is a Gram form to be certified. Six of them are, by 60-digit Cholesky, at half-support 11.52 — the certificates are in Figure 4.

Their conditioning is governed by the node gaps, and here the arithmetic of θ does enter, through the classical three-distance theorem. Reduced modulo Δ₁ the union is a single orbit {mθ mod 1}, so its gaps take at most three values — verified in exact rational arithmetic at all six pairs. What separates them is not the count but the balance. The golden pair's three distances are 2.20 + 3.50 + 5.70 (×10⁻³), a ratio of 2.6; a near-rational's are 0.03 + 79.30 + 79.33, a ratio of 2,644. And the certified pivots track the balance exactly: 4.99×10−6 for the golden pair against 2.76×10−9 for the near-rational, a factor of 1,810.

So the two sides of a pair pull in the same direction after all, but for a different reason than the question supposed. A near-rational θ is bad on the weight side because its ladders overlap, and bad on the conditioning side because its orbit has one tiny gap. The golden ratio is optimal on the second — the most balanced three distances there are — and merely good on the first. The pair that is best on both at once, out of the six certified, is the golden one.

5 · The ceiling, and the answer

Everything above measures what a pair is worth on a finite list of zeros. The question asked something else: whether a pair carries the statement. It does not, and one line of algebra shows why. Write x = γΔ/2. Then

(3) BΔ(γ)  =  Δ·sinc²(γΔ/2)  =  (2/γ)·sin²(x)/x   ⟹   |BΔ(γ)|²  ≤  c₀/γ²,    c₀ = 4·maxx(sin²x/x)².

The maximum sits at the root of tan x = 2x, at x* = 1.165561, giving c₀ = 2.100246 — certified below 2.100246463 by the interval kit, and attained: over 120 meshes × 2000 heights the largest measured |B|²γ² is 2.100245, which is 99.9999% of the bound. The bound contains no Δ. It is a property of the hat, not of any lattice.

The answer

Ceiling. For every Δ > 0 and every γ ≠ 0, |BΔ(γ)|² ≤ c₀/γ² with c₀ = 2.100246. Hence for any family of meshes 𝔉, however large, maxΔ∈𝔉|BΔ(γ)|² ≤ c₀/γ².

Consequence. A pair fixes deletion — the failure mode of rh19 §4 — and nothing fixes starvation. Above γ = 2384 no mesh at all gives a zero even the weight one golden pair guarantees below it; above γ = 14,492 nothing gives weight 10⁻⁸. So (L) at two meshes does not give (W), and enlarging the family cannot change that: the deficiency is not in the lattice, it is in the hat.

Hypotheses, in full. Weil's criterion, cited, for the RH face of rh18's equivalence. IEEE-754 and libmpdec behaving as specified, for the interval-certified c₀ and the 60-digit Cholesky pivots. The ceiling (3) itself is elementary: one substitution and one stationary point, and its constant carries a certified enclosure.

Not proved: RH. Nothing here is a step toward it; the document closes a route rather than opening one. That is worth saying plainly — rh19 ranked this question first because it looked like the cheapest way to strengthen (L), and the honest result is that it is not a way at all.

6 · The bill, audited

38 named checks in 29 seconds. The regression rebuilds rh19's Δ = 0.24 section and its minimum Schur pivot 1.6641×10−5 before any new claim; rh19's Δ* catastrophe is reproduced at 4.563×10−67; the ceiling constant carries both a float value and a certified enclosure, and is checked against 240,000 measured (Δ, r) pairs; the three-distance count is verified in exact rational arithmetic; all six union sections are asserted positive definite; and the two negative results — that Diophantine quality is not the criterion, and that every failure is one of four zeros — each carry their own assertion.

Against the order. "Testing whether a pair of meshes can jointly bound every low-zero weight from below" — done, and the answer is that it can, by 2,857× at a golden pair, on 83.0% of ratios. "A negative answer would be as informative as a positive one" — the negative answer is (3), and it is stronger than the question: it rules out not just pairs but every family, and it does so with a constant rather than an asymptotic.

What is left. rh19's other two successors are untouched and are now the whole list. (ii) A tighter row radius: rh19's ceiling on the certified horizon moves by the log of whatever the arithmetic improves; the two-double logarithm bought a factor 22, a triple-double would buy the next few units of v. Known price, pure engineering. (iii) The floor's decay rate: −0.528, −0.601, −0.639 at Δ = 0.24, 0.12, 0.06 — the rate itself depends on the mesh and nothing explains why. It sits with rh15's σ²v² ≈ 3.9×10⁻³ and rh16's D* as a measured constant of the construction with no derivation behind it, and a derivation would predict rh19's ceiling instead of fitting it.

One new item, and it is the only place (3) leaves room. The ceiling is a statement about this hat. A different window — one whose transform decays more slowly, or not at all on the relevant range — would have a different ceiling, and the arc has never varied the hat. rh12 optimised a window for a different purpose; whether a window exists whose transform holds the high zeros without destroying the sandbox that makes the comb finite is the question (3) actually raises.
