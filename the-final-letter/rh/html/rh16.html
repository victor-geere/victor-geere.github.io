What the structure still had in it

Ordered: before archiving the construction, mine it — not for more certificates of the same kind, but for new uses of the same machinery. Three digs. The first turns Verblunsky's theorem into a procedure and gets the Riemann zeros out of the primes to four decimal places, a thousand times finer than the slice that produced them. The second prices a disproof. The third settles, by building point processes with no arithmetic in them at all, which of this arc's readings were ever about ζ.

1 · The order

Fifteen documents built a geometric object and then dissolved it into two things: a function and a recursion. That is the right end state for a construction — but a construction that has been reduced to its working parts should be run once more, hard, before it is put away. The order for this document was to mine it: to find what the machinery can still do that has not been asked of it.

Three questions, chosen because each has a decisive answer and none is a restatement of the last. (i) Verblunsky's theorem says the Schur sequence is a complete invariant of the measure — the zeros are in the coefficients that the primes produce. Can they be got out? (ii) The criterion is a test; every test has a sensitivity. What size of violation, at what height, would this machine actually catch, and what would catching it cost? (iii) rh15 claimed that the bottom eigenvalue of a slice is the sampling grid rather than ζ. That claim can be settled outright by running the same readings on processes that contain no arithmetic at all.

Part I · The inverse map

2 · The recursion prints the zeros

The Levinson recursion produces, along with the reflection coefficients, a prediction filter A(z) = 1 + ∑aizi — the polynomial that best annihilates the kernel. Its reciprocal is the maximum-entropy (Burg) spectral estimate,

(1) S(θ) = σn²|A(eiθ)|²,    θ = rΔ,

and its poles are the frequencies the filter has locked onto. Since the kernel's frequencies are the zero ordinates, the poles should be the zeros. They are — and not approximately.

Two things have to be said carefully, because the striking number invites overclaiming.

Locating is not detecting. The spectrum has 149 local maxima in that band and only 30 are ordinates; the rest are ripples of the estimate. A rule is needed, and it must not consult the answer. Two zero-blind signals work, and neither works alone: a true line is a pole of the filter, so it sits still when the estimator is disturbed, whereas a ripple is an artefact of one particular fit. So disturb it two independent ways — change the filter order (five orders, majority vote), and change the mesh entirely (the Δ = 0.015 recursion is a different kernel row, a different recursion and a different band). Nothing but a real line survives both. The rule keeps 30 lines, of which 30 are ordinates: precision 1.000, recall 1.000. And the count is independently predicted: Riemann–von Mangoldt gives N(103.7) = θ(T)/π + 1 = 30.65, which also consults no zero. The primes say how many lines to expect and where each one is.

And this is a reconstruction, not a proof. The recursion recovers the ordinates of the measure whose Fourier transform E is — which is what Verblunsky's theorem promises, and it presupposes that E is the transform of a positive measure on the line. That the recovered ordinates are real is a consequence of the computation, not an independent verification of RH. What is genuinely new is the constructive content: the map from primes to zeros, which the explicit formula asserts abstractly, has been run in one direction, numerically, to four decimal places, by an O(n²) recursion on 399 numbers.

One further test, because a spectral estimate can flatter itself. Fit the filter on the kernel over v ≤ 6 only, then free-run it — feed its own output back in — across 398 steps into ground it has never seen. Against the computed kernel on 6 < v ≤ 12 the free-run reproduces it to 16% of the kernel's own rms, and one-step prediction there is exact to 1.7%. The filter is not fitting; it has the frequencies.

Part II · The price, and the provenance

3 · What a disproof would cost

Every criterion is also a detector, and this arc has never asked what its detector's sensitivity is. Take the kernel and move one ordinate off the real axis by δ — the Weil-form image of a zero leaving the critical line — keeping the count:

(2) Eδ(v) = E(v) − 2B(γ)²cos(γv) + 2 Re[B(γ+iδ)² cos((γ+iδ)v)]

which is even in δ, reduces to E at δ = 0, and carries a part growing like eδv. Since a positive-definite function is bounded by its value at 0, Eδ must eventually fail; the question is when. Run the recursion and record k*, the first index with |ρk| ≥ 1.

The obvious estimate is wrong, and instructively so. If detection waited until the growing part reached the kernel's own ceiling E(0), then δv* would be constant at log(E(0)/2B(γ)²) ≈ 4, so δ = 0.01 would need horizon v ≈ 400. The measured horizon at γ₁₀ is 6.25. The recursion is more than fifty times sharper than the boundedness bound — it does not wait for the perturbation to become large, only for it to become inconsistent, and inconsistency is visible against the prediction error σk², which rh15 measured falling like 1/v².

The cost statement follows from what the kernel needs. Reaching horizon v requires the gear comb out to log n = v, i.e. a sieve to ev: every additional unit of horizon multiplies the arithmetic by e. So the detector this arc built, at the sieve it can afford, rules out displacements above 3×10⁻⁴ at the first ordinate and nothing below 0.09 by the hundredth — and pushing the threshold down at high γ is exponentially, not polynomially, expensive. That is a modest claim, but it is the first honest one of its kind here: a criterion is only as good as its sensitivity curve, and this is the curve.

4 · Which readings were ever about ζ

rh15 argued that the bottom eigenvalue of a slice measures the sampling grid — the hat transform's null at r = 2π/Δ — and not the arithmetic. The argument was internal: an energy decomposition and a parameter-free law. It can be settled from outside. Build point processes with the same mean density (1/2π)log(r/2π) as the zeros and no arithmetic whatever — Poisson (exponential gaps), GUE-like (Wigner-surmise gaps), and perfectly rigid (equal gaps), all generated by unfolding through θ(r)/π with a fixed seed — and run the identical readings on each.

That closes rh15's argument from the other side. The eigenvalue was the instrument; the Schur margin is the measurement. And the measurement says something familiar in an unfamiliar coordinate: ζ's margin sits 0.0064 from the GUE-like process and 0.100 from Poisson — sixteen times nearer the one than the other, and nearer GUE than the rigid lattice too (0.0316). Montgomery's pair correlation and Odlyzko's statistics are visible here as a single scalar read off a prime-built recursion. This is a weak test of universality, not a new one — one number, one realisation of each surrogate, no error bars on the surrogates themselves — but it is the right sign, and it is the cheapest form the statement has taken in this arc.

One more thing survives the change of process, and it is worth flagging as an open item rather than a result: rh15's horizon law σk²v² ≈ 3.9×10⁻³ holds for all four processes to within 20% (ζ 4.24×10⁻³, Poisson 3.99, GUE 3.54, rigid 3.52). So that constant, too, is mostly a property of the density, not of the arithmetic — which narrows what an explanation of it would have to explain.

The one-sentence version of the whole arc: a geometric construction was reduced to a single computed function of a single real variable, and the hypothesis to the question of whether one sequence of numbers, generated from the primes by a two-line recursion, ever leaves the unit disc. Nothing in sixteen documents bears on whether it does.

6 · The three moves a successor should make first

(i) Make the certificates unconditional. Everything here is a floating-point statement with a measured error bar (rh15 §6: the kernel moves 1.6×10⁻⁹ absolute between independent quadratures; the Schur margins move ≤ 2.8×10⁻⁶). Redo the same quadrature in interval arithmetic and every computed certificate becomes a theorem about the kernel — a finite amount of engineering and no new mathematics. This is first because it is the only item on the list that is certain to succeed.

(ii) Ask what the ρk are. The Schur coefficients of the Weil kernel are a well-defined arithmetic sequence — computable from Λ(n) and ψ by a finite recursion, complete invariants of the zero measure, O(1) in size, and, as far as this arc knows, never previously written down. Does max|ρ| have a closed form? Do the ρk carry the primes visibly, as E does in its dips at log n? Is the margin 1 − max|ρ| bounded below as Δ → 0 — and if so, by what? A positive answer to the last question would be a genuinely new form of the hypothesis; a negative one would kill the reading. Either is worth more than another certificate.

(iii) Explain the two things this arc measured and could not derive. First: σk²v² ≈ 3.9×10⁻³, stable across three meshes (rh15) and across four point processes (§4 above), so it is a property of the density and the sampling — but its value is unexplained. Second: D*(a, Δ) falls by a decade per mesh halving with no limit in sight, and the natural Nyquist/Levinson-density prediction for where it should settle was tested to Δ = 0.00375 and failed (rh15 §4). One of these is probably a short calculation by someone who knows the Szegő theory properly; the other may not be.

What a successor should not do is cut more slices. That was the arc's instinct for three documents and rh15 showed where it leads: the observable everyone was watching was the instrument. The machinery is cheap now — a certificate costs one more term of one sequence — so the scarce resource is no longer computation. It is knowing which number means something.
