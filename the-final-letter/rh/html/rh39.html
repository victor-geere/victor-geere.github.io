rh39 · the profile

rh30 §10 (a) has been the arc’s only open direction for eight documents: the two-hundred-point family collapses onto one curve in n/Neff, and rh30 asks for a proof. This document does not supply one. It names the profile, refutes the random-phase answer it registered in advance, says what the minimiser actually does — it suppresses rather than annihilates — and finds a second variable rh30 missed, which carries sixty per cent of what rh30 called the width of the collapse. It also reports something uncomfortable: inside the regime where the collapse holds, the celebrated variable buys five per cent over plotting against n.

1 · Where this stands

rh30 §9 proved the arc’s certified points cannot bear on RH whether they pass or fail, and §10 set three directions in their place. rh37 closed (b) by building Beurling–Selberg and measuring both ideas attached to it into the ground. rh38 was a correction to the instrument. That leaves (a): prove the collapse.

The statement is this. Take five windows Δw, five meshes Δm and eight depths n, build the Gram matrix of the zero-side row at each of the two hundred combinations, and plot

y = λmin(n)/E(0) against x = n/Neff(Δw), Neff = ‖μ‖/(2·max weight).

The points lie on one curve. That is the only statement the arc has that is universally quantified over a family, which is why rh30 called it the direction worth proving and why nobody has touched it since.

rh37 §9 named the route: point rh36’s vector method at the arc’s own lattice. That is §4 below. What came out of it was not the result expected, and the sections are ordered so the reader can see the claim weaken and then partly recover.

2 · Regression

Nothing is built on rh30’s numbers before they are reproduced. The five effective counts come back exactly — 7.04, 17.30, 95.73, 206.97, 467.27, a range of 66.34× — and all two hundred λmin/E(0) agree with rh30’s published values to 10−9 relative.

So does the collapse itself. In rh30’s own bins the spread of y is 1.044, 1.423, 1.605 and 1.988 across the four bins covering x < 0.3, which hold 75 of the 200 points; it is 12.796 in [0.3, 1) and 544.97 in [1, 3). The collapse is real, it is a factor of two wide, and it dies at x ≈ 1.

One number rh30 records in passing turns out to matter. The participation ratio (∑w)²/∑w² tops out at 2.363 times Neff and runs down to 1.989 — an 18.8% band across two decades of Neff. rh30 quotes it to argue that Neff is not the count of atoms carrying appreciable weight. It also means the two scales are nearly proportional, which §3 turns on the collapse.

3 · Is Neff the variable?

rh30 bins in x at fixed edges. Comparing a second variable at the same edges is not a comparison, because the two differ by a factor and the bins then hold different points. Score every candidate on the same partition instead: equal-count bins over the 75 points that carry the collapse.

Three results, and none of them is what the headline suggests.

n/Neff gives a geometric-mean spread of 1.6123. n/PART gives exactly the same number, to twelve decimals: PART/Neff never varies enough to reorder the 75 points, so the two are one variable as far as the data can tell. Whatever the collapse establishes, it does not single out Neff over the participation ratio.

And n by itself gives 1.6993. Five per cent worse — 5.40%, to be exact. A deliberately wrong scale, n times the total mass, gives 1.6426, which is better than n alone. Inside the regime where the collapse holds, the variable that makes it a collapse is doing almost no work.

The reason is visible once looked for. x < 0.3 at the widest window would need n < 2.1, and the family starts at n = 4, so the widest window contributes no points at all to the collapsed regime. Across the windows that survive, Neff spans 4.88×, not 66×. rh30’s comparison — against a spread of 1.5×107 at fixed n — is computed over the whole family, where most of the range is x > 1 and the curve has already died. What the collapse mainly establishes is the regime: there is a threshold, and Neff locates it. What happens below the threshold is ordered by n almost as well.

4 · The minimiser

rh36 named the vector that carries λmin on the integer ladder and the answer explained everything around it. rh37 asked for the same on the arc’s own lattice. Here it is.

Write the problem as it really is. With M = G/E(0) = ∑j pj v(xj)v(xj)*, pj = wj/‖μ‖ summing to one, the trace is n and the eigenvalues average to 1, so

y = min over unit c of ⟨|pc|²⟩w / ⟨1⟩w, deg pc = n − 1.

The minimiser is the unit-norm trigonometric polynomial with the smallest weighted mean square over the atoms. Two facts about it, measured at three representative points.

Its coefficient centroid is exactly (n − 1)/2 — to nine decimals at every point tested — and it spreads over about half the lattice, participation 15.10, 12.22 and 28.88 out of 31, 31 and 61. There is no shallow-end concentration of the kind rh36 found on the integer ladder; on an equispaced lattice the minimiser is symmetric, as the symmetry of the problem requires.

The second fact is the useful one. Evaluate |p|² at the eight heaviest atoms of the measure: it sits 232 times below the polynomial’s own weighted mean at the deepest point tested, and below it at every point tested. The minimiser is not spreading its mass evenly and paying the average. It is hunting the heavy atoms and suppressing them, and it suppresses far more of them than it has roots to annihilate.

5 · The annihilator

That suggests a mechanism with the right variable in it, and it is worth building because it half works.

A degree-(n − 1) polynomial has n − 1 roots, and the measure is symmetric under x → −x, so pc can annihilate at most ⌊(n − 1)/2⌋ γ-pairs. Kill the heaviest greedily — put double roots there — and the mass that survives is an explicit upper bound on y. Crucially, the heaviest pair carries exactly 1/Neff of the mass by the definition of Neff, so the bound is a function of x up to the shape of the weight profile.

Built explicitly and evaluated at all 75 points, it is a genuine bound: violated at zero of them. And the mass it kills is x/2 times a shape factor running from 0.500 at the sparsest up to 0.965 at the densest — so Neff really is the right normalisation for annihilable mass, and that is where the collapse variable comes from.

But as a profile it is loose, rms log residual 0.2484, worse than every candidate in §6 including the trivial ones. §4 says why: annihilation is the wrong model. A polynomial that puts thirty double roots on the thirty heaviest pairs has spent all its freedom on thirty atoms; the true minimiser instead pushes hundreds of atoms two orders of magnitude down without zeroing any of them, and pays less. The variable is explained; the curve is not.

F(x) = 1 − x, with a median ratio of 0.9931 — unbiased to seven parts in a thousand — and an rms log residual of 0.1559, which is about ±17%. Given that the within-bin spread is a factor of two, that is as well as any single-variable curve can be expected to do, and the first three candidates are not cleanly separated by this data.

The registered prediction was that Marchenko–Pastur would fail, and it fails. If the ordinates behaved like random phases, M would be a weighted sample covariance matrix and the hard edge (1 − √x)² would be the profile. It scores 0.6490, four times the winner, and it is low by a median factor of 1.67 and a worst of 3.76. Low is the informative direction: the true minimum eigenvalue is much larger than random phases would give, which is what rigidity of the zeros predicts and what rh36 saw from the other side when it found the floor to be a suppression factor below chance. The zeros are not a random sample, and the collapse profile is not a random-matrix law.

7 · The second variable

rh30 treats the within-bin spread as the width of the collapse. It is not noise. At x = 0.2947 the five meshes give 0.536, 0.576, 0.720, 0.785, 0.576 — structure, not scatter.

Divide out the profile and average the residual within each octave of the mesh-to-window ratio. It peaks at 1.042 where the mesh matches the window and falls away on both sides, to 0.734 three octaves below and 0.881 four octaves above.

That single variable carries 60.0% of the residual variance. It is genuinely the ratio and not either scale: the mesh alone carries 35.9% and the window alone 18.3%. Dividing it out takes the rms log residual from 0.1559 to 0.0986 and narrows every quantile bin, the widest from 1.988 to 1.567.

The reading is natural. The window sets the measure’s bandwidth and the mesh sets the sampling rate at which the Gram matrix reads it; matching them is the least redundant sampling, and mismatching either way wastes degrees of freedom. rh30’s family varied both, saw the residual, and booked it as the collapse’s width. Most of it is a second axis.

8 · Where this leaves the conjunction

Unmoved. rh30 §10 (a) asked for a proof of the collapse and there is no proof here. What there is, stated without inflation:

The profile has a name and a measured error bar — F(x) = 1 − x at ±17%, unbiased. The random-matrix answer is refuted by a prediction registered in advance, and the direction of its failure identifies rigidity as the cause. The minimiser has been named on the arc’s own lattice, and what it does is suppression rather than annihilation, which is why every bound the arc has built from annihilation or from worst cases is loose. The variable’s origin is explained — 1/Neff is the heaviest pair’s share of the mass, and x/2 is the annihilable fraction. And the collapse has a second axis carrying most of its width.

Set against that, one honest deflation: inside the regime where it holds, the collapse is 5.4% better than plotting against n, and it cannot distinguish Neff from the participation ratio at all. rh30 stated (a) as the arc’s one universally quantified claim. It is still that, but a proof of it would be a proof of something weaker than the phrase “collapses onto a single curve” suggests, and any future attempt should carry §3’s numbers alongside rh30’s.

The certified set stays at nine, renewed once more.

9 · What this document leaves

(a) The threshold, not the curve.
§3 relocates what is worth proving. Below x ≈ 1 the ordering is nearly trivial; at x ≈ 1 something happens, and that is where Neff earns its keep. The sharp statement to go after is the death: why λmin/E(0) falls off a cliff when the degree passes the effective atom count, and whether the cliff is at Neff, at PART, or between them. The family as built cannot separate those; a family with windows chosen to spread PART/Neff could.
(b) The suppression bound.
§4 says the minimiser suppresses hundreds of atoms rather than annihilating ⌊(n−1)/2⌋. The inequality that would capture that is the same shape as the one rh37 §7 named — a joint constraint across many atoms rather than one at a time. Two documents have now arrived at that from opposite directions, which is the first time the arc has had two independent routes to one missing lemma.
(c) The second axis is free data.
The mesh-to-window ratio is a knob rh30's family already varied. Any future family should record it, and any collapse claimed without it is claiming credit for a residual that has a name.
(d) The certified set stays closed.
Nine, unchanged, renewed once more.
